Fan-facing analytics and broadcast

Everything so far has been for eleven people in a room. Tonight the audience is forty thousand in a stadium and a few million more on a broadcast feed, and none of them signed up for a modelling lecture. The single hardest communication job in sport is telling a fan that the chance their striker just missed was a 0.03 chance, three seconds after it went in. You are not going to win that argument by being right. You win it by making the graphic say what the model actually claims, with the denominator on it, the sample size on it, and one clause about what the number does not mean. Rivermouth's content team publishes four graphics a week and has never once asked an analyst to check one. That changes tonight.

Part 1 · the hardest job in sports communication

Explaining a probability to someone who is angry 15 min live

A shot is taken. The graphic flashes 0.03. The ball hits the net. Within a minute your club account has four hundred replies telling you the model is garbage, and about half of them are making the same mistake in three different costumes. The mistake is not stupidity. It is that the graphic said one thing, the model claimed another, and nobody ever closed the gap between them. Closing that gap is your job, not the fan's.

LiveThe three misreadings you will meet every week5 min▶

Almost every angry reply is one of three things wearing a different jersey. Learn to name them fast, because the reply you write depends entirely on which one it is.

  • xG as a scoreline that should have happened. "We won the xG 2.4 to 0.8" is treated as a moral verdict on the result. It is not a scoreline. It is a summary of the chances created, which is a different and more useful thing, and it does not entitle anyone to a result.
  • One match read as if it meant something. Over a single match, xG is noisy - it is a sample-size instrument, and a season is where it starts earning its keep. Anyone quoting one match's figure as evidence about a player or a system is reading weather as climate.
  • A model output read as a judgement on a person. A 0.03 chance says nothing about the striker's ability, character or contract. It describes the chance, not the man who took it. This is the misreading that does real damage, and it is the one your graphic is most likely to have invited.

The honest framing, which you can say on air without embarrassment: post-match expected goals predicted match outcome at around 0.656 accuracy in a peer-reviewed Bundesliga comparison. That is genuinely informative and nowhere near certainty. Both halves of that sentence matter. Drop the first half and you sound like you are apologising for your own work; drop the second half and you have just promised something you cannot deliver.

The one sentence, ready to use "That was a hard chance - shots from there go in about three times in a hundred, so we are delighted it went in, and it does not mean he is a poor finisher or that the model is broken. Over a season the number tells you a lot; over one shot it tells you almost nothing."
LiveWhy "being right" loses the room4 min▶

The instinct when a fan says the model is garbage is to explain the model. It never works, for a reason worth understanding: they are not disputing your maths, they are disputing that a number gets to comment on something they just watched with their own eyes.

  • Concede the event first. The goal happened. It counted. Say so before you say anything else, and mean it.
  • Then narrow the claim. The number is about the chance, not the outcome, and not the player.
  • Then give the sample. Numbers get their authority from how many times they have been checked. If you do not say how many, the number is just an opinion in a nicer font.
  • Then say what it does not mean, explicitly. This is the step everybody skips and it is the step that does the work.

This is the same move as b8's one-slide rule. A coach who has never opened a dashboard and a fan on a phone at half-time have exactly the same requirement: one idea, stated plainly, with its limits attached.

Self-studyWhat a probability is, in language nobody objects to3 min read▶

A probability is a long-run bookkeeping statement. It says: across everything we have seen that looks like this, here is how often it ended a particular way. Three consequences follow, and they are worth having ready.

  • It is never wrong about one event. A 0.03 chance that goes in was still a 0.03 chance. Rare things happen at their rate, which is the whole point of quoting a rate.
  • It inherits its features. A model without defensive pressure will overrate crowded chances every time. When a fan asks what is in the model, that is a good question, not an attack.
  • It gets better with volume and worse with anecdote. Adjusting a model because one improbable thing happened is how you dismantle it one weekend at a time.

The version that survives contact with an audience avoids the word "expected" entirely. "About three in a hundred" lands. "An xG of 0.03" starts a fight.

Part 2 · build-along

Graphics that inform rather than mislead ★ 22 min · everyone builds one

There are five ways a public graphic goes wrong, and Rivermouth's content team has shipped all five in the last two months. None of them were dishonest. Every one of them was a number that had been separated from the thing that made it meaningful.

LiveThe five failures, named5 min▶
  • A percentage with no denominator. "68% pressing success" could be 34 of 50 or 2 of 3. Those are different worlds and the graphic hides which one you are in.
  • A season metric shown after one match. Some numbers only mean anything at volume. Publishing them after ninety minutes is not a shortcut, it is a category error.
  • A cumulative xG line read as the story of the match. A step chart is a record of chances in time order. It is not a narrative, and the team whose line ends higher did not win a moral victory.
  • A cross-league player comparison with no adjustment. b7 spent a whole session on this. The same output in two leagues is not the same performance, and publishing the raw pair invites a conclusion the data does not support.
  • Any graphic with no uncertainty on it at all. A number rendered in a confident typeface with no range, no sample and no caveat is making a claim of certainty whether or not anyone typed the word.

What works is unglamorous and takes about forty seconds: counts with denominators, a sample-size label, the date range, and one clause saying what the number does not mean. Four things. They are the same four things b6 made you attach to a tendency before you were allowed to call it verified.

Real world

The evidence base does not support certainty, and saying so is not weakness. Post-match xG predicted match outcome at around 0.656 accuracy in a peer-reviewed comparison. Commercial shot-quality models train on close to a million historical shots across twenty-plus variables, and they still produce a probability rather than a verdict. If the most heavily resourced version of this work reports uncertainty, a graphic from a mid-table club's content account has no business implying it has none.

Take one match's shot data. Write down the raw pair first: total shots for each side, and the model value attached to each shot. No graphic yet.

Choose one idea. Not three. "Their chances were volume, ours were quality" is one idea. "Here is our full attacking profile" is not.

Convert every percentage back into a count with its denominator. If you cannot recover the denominator, the number does not go on the graphic.

Add the sample-size label and the date range in the same visual block as the number, not in a corner at 8px. Small print is a way of technically saying it.

Write the "does not mean" clause. One sentence. For a shot map: "this does not say who deserved to win".

Hand it to someone who does not work in analytics and ask them what it claims. If their answer is bigger than your claim, the graphic is not finished.

✓ Prompt that produces a publishable caption 
You are writing a caption for a public graphic for a football club.

METRIC: [name]  VALUE: [count] of [denominator]
SAMPLE: [n matches]  WINDOW: [first date] to [last date]

Write 3 sentences, max 45 words total.
1. State the metric as a count out of its denominator.
2. State the sample size and the date range explicitly.
3. State one specific thing this number does NOT mean.

Rules: no superlatives, no league ranking claims, no comparison
to another league. Do not imply certainty & do not use the words
"proves", "shows conclusively", "guarantees" or "deserved".
If the denominator is missing, reply "denominator required" only.
✗ Prompt that produces something you will have to delete 
Give me a punchy stat for social from this match.
Why the second one fails "Punchy" is an instruction to remove the denominator, because the denominator is the part that makes a number less punchy. You have asked for the failure mode by name. Anything that comes back will be a bare percentage with no sample, and you will publish it because it sounds good.
Part 3 · the rails

What you may publish, and what stays inside read and keep

A public output is a different object from an internal one, and the difference is not tone. Once a number leaves the building it can be traded on, quoted back at a player, or used to argue that someone should lose their job. These four cards are the constraints that come with the audience.

LiveIntegrity rails for anyone producing public outputs4 min▶

Three rules, and they are not negotiable regardless of how good the content idea is.

  • Predictive material about your own fixtures is sensitive before official team news. A graphic that reveals expected availability, a likely shape, or a modelled probability for a match you are about to play is information the market does not have. It waits until the club has published team news through the official channel.
  • Inside information stays inside. Training-ground observations, medical status, and anything from the wearable or medical systems in b5 are not content. Not anonymised, not "hinted at", not as a teaser.
  • Wagering is explicitly out of scope for this course. This track does not teach betting strategy, betting models, or how to find an edge, and no session will. What you get here is the integrity rail: know that predictive material can move a market, and behave accordingly. The commercial and regulatory side of that sits with director session a5, which is where the club-level policy belongs.

If you are unsure whether something crosses a rail, the test is not "is this interesting". It is "could someone act on this before the market has it". If yes, it waits, and you ask the person whose job that actually is.

LiveWhen the graphic is not yours to control3 min▶

Broadcasters build their own graphics from their own data, on their own deadline, and your name is nowhere near the render. You still get asked about it on Monday. What you can do:

  • Supply the caption, not just the number. If you hand over a figure, hand over the denominator and the sample size in the same message. Numbers travel; context only travels if you attach it.
  • Ask which model produced it. Providers differ on features. A value from a model without defensive pressure is not the same object as yours, and reconciling two numbers on air is a losing position.
  • Agree in advance what you will not confirm. Availability, medical status, and anything about a decision that has not been made.
  • Do not correct a broadcaster live over a rounding difference. Correct the misreading, never the decimal. The misreading is what causes damage.
LivePublishing a metric about a named individual3 min▶

The moment a public metric is about one person's performance, you have taken on a duty of care that does not apply to a team-level chart. The player did not consent to a model, cannot reply on equal terms, and will read it.

  • Say what the metric is not. An underperformance figure against shot quality is a statement about a sample of chances, not about temperament, effort or worth. Put that clause on the graphic.
  • Check the sample before the phrasing. A player-level number over a handful of matches should generally not be published at all. Over one match it should never be.
  • Route it through the people who own player relations. This is not analytics being timid. It is that the consequences land on someone else and they get a say.
  • Never publish anything derived from medical or wearable systems about an individual. b5 is unambiguous about this, and it is orientation on how to work alongside clinicians, not medical advice.
Self-studyFans want more depth than you think3 min read▶

The default assumption in club content is that the audience wants everything simplified. The evidence from how supporters actually engage points the other way: a large part of the audience already reads shot maps, argues about sample size, and knows which providers use which features. Simplifying past that point does not protect them, it insults them, and it removes exactly the context that stops a number being misread.

  • Explaining the method builds trust; hiding it builds suspicion. A short line on what is in the model converts hostile replies into arguments about football, which is a much better place to be.
  • Depth is cheap to add and expensive to retrofit. A denominator costs six characters. A correction costs a week.
  • The people who complain loudest about numbers usually want better numbers, not fewer. Treat the complaint as a feature request.

The club that publishes "34 of 50, across six matches, and this does not rank us in the league" ends up with a more informed audience and considerably less to apologise for.

Homework

Try it yourself - this week ◐ 30-40 min total

  • Take one graphic your club published in the last month and rewrite it with a denominator and a sample-size label. Put the two versions side by side and show the content team.
  • Write, in one sentence, the explanation of a shot-quality number you would give to an angry fan. Read it out loud. If it contains the word "expected", rewrite it.
  • Go through the last month of your club's public output and check whether anything published before official team news could have moved a betting market. Write down what you find and take it to whoever owns integrity policy.
  • Find one published graphic that compares players across leagues with no adjustment. Work out what claim it is actually inviting, and what the adjusted version would say instead.
  • Ask one person in the content team what they think xG means. Do not correct them in the moment. Write down the gap between their answer and the model's claim, because that gap is your next graphic brief.

They are not disputing your maths, they are disputing that a number gets to comment on something they just watched. Explaining the model reads as a defence; conceding the event and then narrowing the claim reads as an answer. The 0.656 figure is honest framing, not a trump card - it is informative and nowhere near certainty.

The audience understands pressing perfectly well; what they cannot see is how many sequences the number came from, or over how many matches.
