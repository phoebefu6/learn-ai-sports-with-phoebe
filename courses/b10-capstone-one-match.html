Capstone: one match, one report

Nine sessions produced nine artifacts: a data dictionary, a shot-quality model with two numbers attached, a six-clip pack, an availability picture that refuses to be an injury prediction, three verified tendencies with counts and denominators, an adjusted longlist, and one slide. Tonight they become a single match report for a head coach who has never opened a dashboard, at a club whose medical department still does not entirely trust you. Then you score it - twelve rows, eight of which are points and four of which are stops. A report that fails a stop does not get a low score. It does not get a score at all until it is fixed. And before you leave, the model gate one last time: two numbers, always.

Part 0

What tonight is, and what it is not

This is not a new lesson. It is the assembly and the audit. You take one real match at Rivermouth FC, walk it through Capture, Model, Judge, Communicate, Decide, and produce the deliverable the whole track has been building toward: one slide a coach will read, plus an appendix that exists to answer exactly one question. Then you score it honestly, including the parts where it fails. The most valuable thing you take out of this room is not the report. It is the habit of scoring your own work before someone else does.

Part 1 · assembly

Assemble the report 13 min live

Walk the match through all five stages of the loop and name which session produced each piece. Every stage has an artifact you already own, and every stage has material you are about to throw away. The throwing away is the work.

LiveThe five stages, walked through one match7 min▶
  • Capture (b2). Start with the data dictionary, because the first question anyone asks is what a row means. State plainly which claims came from event data and which needed tracking. Defender pressure is a tracking feature; if you only have event data for this fixture, say so on the appendix page rather than letting the reader assume.
  • Model (b3). The shot-quality read on both teams' chances. One number goes on the slide and it never travels alone - fitted and held out, together, in the same line. This is the discipline the whole track has been drilling and the slide is where it gets tested.
  • Judge (b5, b6). Two artifacts. The availability and exposure picture, which is a planning input and explicitly not an injury prediction. And the three verified tendencies from b6, each carrying its count, its denominator and its date range. Judge is also where two of the three tendencies quietly get demoted, because two fit on a slide and three do not.
  • Communicate (b8, b9). The one-slide rule. One idea, stated in the coach's language, with the uncertainty in the same sentence as the number. If any of this becomes public material, b9's four elements apply and the integrity rails apply with them.
  • Decide. Not yours. The coach picks a team, a clinician decides return to play, a director signs a contract. Your report is a supportive input and the slide should read like one.

The finished object is one slide plus an appendix, and the appendix exists to answer exactly one question - the one you know the coach is going to ask when he sees the slide. Not a general reference. One question. If you cannot predict the question, you do not know the room well enough yet, and that is the finding.

Real world

Why the appendix is capped at one question. A coach who has never opened a dashboard will not read a twelve-page pack, and building one signals that you could not decide what mattered. Rivermouth's three analysts have finite hours; every page in the appendix is a page not spent on next week's opposition. The cap is not minimalism for style. It is what makes the department sustainable at three people.

Self-studyFour lines, and what to do when one is not ready4 min read▶

Four lines is a budget, not a suggestion: the one thing to do differently on Saturday, two verified tendencies each carrying a count and a denominator, and the availability note. The honest version of this capstone is that at least one of them will be weaker than you would like. That is normal and it has a correct handling.

  • A tendency that failed its sample check does not get softened, it gets dropped. "There is a slight suggestion that" is how a department loses its credibility one hedge at a time.
  • A model number without a held-out partner does not go on the slide. Produce the second number or remove the first one. There is no third option.
  • An availability picture with gaps says where the gaps are. Missing data is a finding, and naming it is how the medical department starts to trust you.
  • If two of the four lines are weak, ship a two-line slide. A short honest report beats a full-length one padded to look complete.
Part 2 · the audit

The report scorecard ◐ 16 min live · score your own

Twelve rows. Eight of them are points, and you are aiming for eight of eight. Four of them are rails, and rails are stops rather than points - a report that fails a rail does not get a low score, it does not get a score at all until the failure is fixed. This distinction is the entire reason the list works.

#RowPasses whenType
1Two numbers reported, not oneEvery model figure on the slide appears with its held-out partner in the same line. b1's discipline, applied to a real deliverable.Point
2Features justifiedYou can say in one sentence why each feature is in the model and what it is measuring about the game rather than about the squad.Point
3No identity or reputation featureNothing in the model encodes who took the shot, their market value or their name. The anti-feature raises the flattering number and lowers the real one.Point
4Sample size statedEvery claim on the slide carries the number of matches, sequences or events it rests on, visibly, not in small print.Point
5One idea, not fiveA reader can say what the report wants after five seconds, and their answer matches yours.Point
6Counts with denominatorsNo bare percentages anywhere. 17 of 24, not 71%. If the denominator is unrecoverable, the claim comes off.Point
7A date range on every claimEach tendency and each metric says which window it covers, so nobody has to guess whether it includes last season.Point
8One thing to do differentlyThe slide ends with a concrete action for the next match, not a theme, an observation or a summary.Point
9No selection recommendation from a modelNothing in the report tells a coach who to pick. Models inform; a named human decides and carries it.STOP
10No return-to-play viewThe report expresses no opinion, direct or implied, on whether a player is fit to play. That call belongs to a clinician.STOP
11No medical detailNo diagnoses, no treatment status, no injury specifics. Availability and exposure only, and this is orientation rather than medical advice.STOP
12Nothing publishable that could move a marketNo predictive material about your own fixtures leaves the building before official team news. b9 covers the rails; a5 owns the policy.STOP
Why four of the twelve are stops A points system quietly says every row is tradeable - lose one here, make it up there. Three of these rows touch a person's body, livelihood or career, and the fourth touches the integrity of a competition. None of them can be offset by a well-drawn chart. Making them stops removes the negotiation entirely, which is the only thing that makes them hold under deadline pressure.
LiveScoring honestly when it is your own work4 min▶

Self-scoring fails in predictable ways. Watch for these three, because you will do all of them at least once.

  • Awarding row 4 because the sample size is in your head. If it is not on the slide, the row fails. The reader cannot see your working memory.
  • Reading row 9 too narrowly. "The model prefers this shape" is a selection recommendation wearing a tactical hat. If a coach could reasonably read the slide as telling him who to play, the rail is failed.
  • Passing row 6 on a rounded count. "About 7 in 10" is a percentage with extra steps. Give the pair.

The strongest version of this exercise is to score someone else's report and have them score yours. Ten minutes each, and it will do more for the department's output than a month of tooling.

Part 3 · the final gate

The two-number gate, one last time ★ 8 min · everyone runs it

This is not a new lesson. It is a gate. The model below is loaded with all five legitimate features, exactly as your report's shot-quality number should be, and it shows this season 85.0% and next season 83.0%, a gap of 2.0 points. That is the shape of a number that has earned a place on a slide. Before you close the track, break it once more and watch what you would have shipped.

Read the loaded state first: 85.0 fitted, 83.0 held out, gap 2.0. Say both numbers out loud in the order you would say them to a coach.

Take one feature off and put it back. Watch both numbers move together. A model learning the game moves both.

Now switch ⚠ Shooter reputation on one last time and read BOTH numbers: 96.0 fitted, 77.0 held out, gap 19.0.

The gap went from 2.0 to 19.0. The flattering number rose eleven points while the number that matters fell six. This is row 3 of the scorecard, live.

Switch it back off. Then write on your slide draft the exact line you will use: the metric, the fitted number, the held-out number, and the sample. One line.

The gate, stated plainly If your report contains a model number and you cannot say the second number without looking it up, the report is not finished. That is the entire test, and it is the one habit that would have caught most of the mistakes in this track before they reached a coach.
Part 4 · after the track

Monday, and everything this course did not teach read and keep

Ten sessions is enough to make you dangerous in a useful way and nowhere near enough to make you finished. These four cards are the handover.

LiveThe first Monday back3 min▶

Change three things in week one, not thirty. The department that changes everything at once gets rolled back by February.

  • Add the second number to one recurring output. Whichever report your department sends most often. Nothing else about it changes.
  • Put denominators on everything that goes outside the analytics room. Internal decks, coach packs, public graphics. It costs minutes and it changes how your numbers are argued with.
  • Take one thing off the coach pack. The page nobody has ever asked a question about. Watch whether anyone notices, then take another.
  • Ask the medical lead one question and do not argue with the answer. The b1 homework, still the highest-return thing on this list at a club where they do not trust you yet.
LiveWhat this course deliberately did not teach3 min▶

Knowing the edges of your competence is part of the competence. Five things are out of scope by design, and none of them are gaps you should quietly fill in yourself.

  • Medical advice and return-to-play protocols. A clinician owns those. Everything in b5 and here is orientation on working alongside them, not a substitute for them.
  • Legal advice on athlete data. Consent, employment and transfer regulation belong with counsel and with player representation. Director session a4 frames the club-level questions.
  • Betting strategy of any kind. Integrity rails are covered in b9 and a5. Wagering, edges and betting models are not taught here and will not be.
  • Production data infrastructure. Pipelines, warehousing and platform engineering are a different discipline and a different course.
  • Sport-specific coaching methodology. The worked examples use football because the public evidence base is deepest there. The pattern transfers; the coaching does not come from you.
LiveThe one habit worth keeping2 min▶

If everything else fades, keep this one: report two numbers, always.

It is ten seconds of work and it catches the failure mode that looks exactly like success. It caught the reputation feature, where the fitted number climbed to 96.0 while the held-out number fell to 77.0. It will catch the next version of that mistake, which will arrive dressed differently and will still be the model learning something about this squad rather than about the game.

It also does something less obvious. A department that always reports two numbers is a department that has visibly chosen honesty over the flattering figure, and that reputation is the thing that eventually gets you into the room where selection is discussed. You do not get there by being impressive. You get there by being reliable about what you will not claim.

Self-studyIf you work with directors, point them at the a-track2 min read▶

This track is built for the people producing analysis. The six director sessions cover the same evidence base from the side of the people who fund and govern it, and several of the questions you will get stuck on are theirs to answer rather than yours.

  • a2 - the data estate. Who owns event, tracking, wearable, medical, video and ticketing data, and who may see each.
  • a4 - athlete welfare and data rights. The consent and representation questions behind everything in b5.
  • a5 - fans, commercial and integrity. Where the betting-integrity rails around any predictive output actually get set as policy.
  • a6 - building the analytics function. Staffing, coach adoption and proving value without overclaiming, which is the organisational version of this capstone.

Handing a director the right session is faster than winning the argument yourself, and it makes the rails somebody else's policy rather than your personal preference.

Homework

Try it yourself - this week ◐ 45-60 min total

  • Take one report your department actually sent in the last month and run it through all twelve rows. Score the eight, check the four rails, and write the score down before you start fixing anything.
  • Fix every failure. If a rail failed, fix it first and do not record a score until it passes.
  • Write the one habit on a card and put it where you write reports: two numbers, always.
  • Ask your coach what he did with the last report you sent. Not what he thought of it - what he did. Write the answer down verbatim.
  • Swap reports with one colleague and score each other's. Ten minutes each, out loud, with the twelve rows in front of you.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · A report scores 8 of 8 on the scored rows but implies a preferred starting eleven based on the model. What is the score?

Rails are stops rather than points precisely so they cannot be traded against good work elsewhere. A model does not make a selection call, and "the model prefers this shape" is a selection recommendation wearing a tactical hat. Fix it, then score.

2 · The simulator shows 85.0 this season and 83.0 next season. You switch reputation on and this season reads 96.0. What do you report?

The gap moved from 2.0 to 19.0. The model learned who takes the shots rather than what makes a shot good, and next August the squad changes. This is exactly the failure the two-number habit exists to catch, and it is row 3 of the scorecard.

3 · What is the correct shape of the finished match report?

The appendix is not optional and it is not a library - it answers the one question you know the slide will provoke. The pile of discarded material should be far larger than what ships, and if it is not, no judging has happened yet. Deleting the working entirely is the opposite mistake: you need it to defend the four lines.
