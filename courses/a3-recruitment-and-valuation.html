Part 1 · the split that decides the window

Models and scouts do different jobs 15 min live

The argument about data versus scouting is a category error, and it costs clubs money every window. The two are good at different halves of the same problem. A model is very good at searching a space no scout could physically watch, and at finding players whose underlying performance is not reflected in their outcomes - which is precisely the job xG-style metrics are now widely used for in recruitment. A scout is very good at everything a data feed cannot see: character, coachability, how a player behaves at 3-0 down, whether he will move country and settle, whether his family will settle with him.

LiveTwo failure modes, and the two things a model genuinely does7 min▶

Every club you compete with sits somewhere on a line between two ways of getting this wrong, and neither end is a strategy.

  • Pure data recruitment buys profiles. The numbers say the player fits the role. Nobody asked whether he fits the dressing room, the language, the weather, the coach, or the wage structure. The signing is defensible on a slide and unusable on a Tuesday.
  • Pure scouting watches 40 players when the space contains 4,000. Your scouts are good. They are also finite. Whatever list they bring you is the list of players they were already able to see, which is not the same thing as the list of players who would help you.
  • What the model is genuinely for, one: scale of search. Four thousand players in the leagues you can realistically buy from, and three analysts. No amount of scouting effort covers that. A model covers it in an afternoon and hands back a screened list your scouts then spend their limited hours on.
  • And two: underlying performance versus outcomes. A striker with six goals may have generated chances worth far more than six, and one with fourteen may have generated chances worth far fewer. xG-style metrics are now widely used in recruitment for exactly this.

The working arrangement is boring and it works: the model does the searching and the adjusting, the scouts do the watching and the judging, and the two meet at the shortlist. If your process has no stage where they meet, you do not have a process, you have two departments.

Real world

Scale is what separates a real model from a spreadsheet. Commercial shot-quality models train on close to a million historical shots across twenty-plus variables including distance, angle, defensive pressure and goalkeeper location. That is the machinery underneath the recruitment metric you are being sold. It is genuinely impressive, and it still tells you nothing about whether the player will settle in a new country. Both halves of that sentence are the session.

Part 2 · the adjustments before you trust a list

Where the numbers mislead 12 min live

A raw recruitment number is almost never comparable to another raw recruitment number, and the gap between the raw version and the adjusted version is often the whole decision. Five things move a ranking: league level, sample size, age, role, and survivorship. If your analyst has not told you which of these were applied, the list you are holding has not been adjusted at all.

LiveThe five adjustments, in the order they bite6 min▶
AdjustmentWhat it fixesThe question to ask your analyst
League levelA number produced against weaker opposition is not comparable to one produced against stronger opposition. It is not a small correction.What discount did you apply, and where did the discount come from?
Sample sizeSix hundred minutes is roughly seven matches. A per-90 rate on that will swing wildly and settle almost nothing.How many minutes, and how wide is the range around this number?
Age curveThe same output at 21 and at 31 carries opposite implications for what you are buying and for resale.Is this rate rising, flat or falling, and against which curve?
Positional roleTwo players carry the same label and do different jobs. Comparing them on a striker metric compares nothing.What role did you assign him, and does the video agree with the label?
SurvivorshipYou only ever observe outcomes for players somebody signed. The ones nobody signed have no track record, which is not the same as a bad one.What is missing from this data, and who is invisible in it?
LiveThe reputation anti-feature, and why it is worse in recruitment5 min▶

Two ideas collide here. The first is the two-number discipline: never accept one accuracy figure. Ask for two - how the model performs on the data it was fitted to, and how it performs on data it has never seen - and watch the gap between them. A small gap means the model learned something about football. A large gap means it learned something about one specific set of players.

The second is the reputation anti-feature. When a model is allowed to use who a player IS - his name, his profile, his market value, the club he plays for - rather than what he DOES, the flattering number goes up and the useful number goes down. In the shot model demonstrated in session b1, switching that feature on raises the fitted figure to 96.0 while the held-out figure falls to 77.0, widening the gap from 2 points to 19. The model got worse and the headline improved.

Now apply that to recruitment, where it is not an academic problem. A model that has learned who a player is will rank the famous players highest. That is the exact opposite of the value recruitment analytics is supposed to add. You are paying for a search of four thousand players and you are getting back the forty names your scouts could have written on a napkin, with decimal places attached and an aura of rigour that makes them harder to argue with.

Real world

The honest nuance, because player identity is not always wrong. Properly pooled player effects, done with the shrinkage that Bayesian hierarchical approaches apply, genuinely improve season-level prediction for individual players and teams over a plain model. The failure is the naive version: raw identity and reputation, unregularised, on a small sample. When a vendor says the model accounts for the player, the follow-up question is how, not whether.

Part 3 · the director's job

Building the shortlist ◐ 10 min live + self-study

The rest is what only you can do: set the brief, hold the line on bias, protect your scouts' judgment without letting it become a veto, and remember at every step that valuation sits on the decision line. Models shortlist. People sign.

LiveBias: a model trained on who was signed reproduces who was signed4 min▶

This is the most common serious flaw in a recruitment model and it is almost never described honestly by the people selling it. If the training target is did this signing work out, the training data is by definition the set of players somebody already chose to sign. Every preference that shaped those choices - nationality, league of origin, agent relationships, physical profile, the kind of player that particular club has always liked - is now baked into the model as a pattern to reproduce.

  • Survivorship makes it worse. Players nobody signed generate no outcome data, so the model never learns that some of them would have been fine. Absence of evidence arrives looking like evidence of absence. So does a model trained partly on transfer fees: it has learned market consensus, which is the thing you were trying to beat.
  • What to ask. What was the target variable, what population did the training data come from, and which groups of players are underrepresented in it. If nobody can answer, the model is not ready for your window, and a model that reproduces your past preferences cannot beat clubs with more money.
LiveValuation crosses the decision line3 min▶

The decision line is the spine of this whole course: three calls a model must never make on its own - selection, return to play, and valuation. Recruitment lives on the third one, and directors sometimes treat it as the soft one because no medical file is involved. It is not soft. A valuation moves a person's career, their income, where their children go to school, and whether they are still in the game in three years.

  • Models shortlist. People sign. There is no version of this where a number is the decision.
  • A named human owns each signing. Not the committee, not the model, not the tool. A name, written down, before the fee is agreed.
LiveHow to brief an analyst so you get a shortlist, not a ranking4 min▶

Most bad recruitment analysis is caused by a bad brief. "Find me a striker" produces a leaderboard, because that is the only thing an unconstrained question can produce. A good brief has six parts and takes four minutes to write.

  1. The role, described as a job. Not "striker" but what he has to do in your system, in your league, next to your existing players.
  2. The constraints. Budget, wage ceiling, age band, contract situation, and the leagues you can realistically buy from.
  3. The adjustments you require, plus a minimum minutes threshold. League, minutes, age, role, named so the answer has to address them. Say the minutes threshold out loud, or you will be handed a 400-minute wonder.
  4. The output format: a longlist with reasons. Twenty to forty names, each with two lines saying why it is here and what the doubt is. Not a ranked table.
  5. What would change the answer. Ask the analyst to state the limitations of their own work as part of the deliverable.
LiveWhat to tell a scout whose favourite is not on the list3 min▶
  • Start by agreeing the list is incomplete. It is. It was built from adjusted numbers on a screened population and it does not contain character, injury context, or what happened in a match nobody has data for.
  • Ask what he sees that the data cannot. Then write it down as a specific claim, not a feeling. "He organises the press without being told" is a claim. "I just like him" is not, and the scout usually has the first underneath the second. Add the player with that reason attached: a named exception is a healthy process, a quiet override is not.
  • Then check why the model missed him. Often it is a data gap, a role label, or a minutes threshold, and that is a finding worth more than the individual player. Never let the disagreement be settled by whoever brought a slide.
Self-studySelling, not just buying, and the metrics that flag a decline5 min read▶

Clubs at your level make more money from selling well than from buying well, and almost all recruitment analytics attention goes to the buying half. The selling question is a different one: is this player's output about to fall behind his reputation, and is that the moment to trade.

  • Look for the gap opening the other way. Underlying performance running below outcomes over a meaningful sample is the same instrument you use to find bargains, pointed at your own squad. Reputation lags reality in both directions, and that lag is the commercial opportunity.
  • Watch the physical exposure trend, carefully. Declining minutes, declining involvement in high-intensity phases, and a rising availability problem are context, not a verdict, and this is an area where session a4's rails apply in full.
Good - a longlist you can argue with Build a candidate LONGLIST for the following role. Do not rank. ROLE: wide forward, left side, mid-block counter-attacking system. Must defend the full-back's outside shoulder and carry from deep. CONSTRAINTS: age 21-25, contract expiring within 24 months, tier 2 and tier 3 leagues in markets we can actually buy from. MINIMUM SAMPLE: 1,200 league minutes this season. Exclude anyone below it and tell me how many candidates that exclusion removed. REQUIRED ADJUSTMENTS - apply all four and show the number before and after each one: league level, per-90 minutes normalisation, age curve, and positional role assigned from on-pitch behaviour, not the listed position. DO NOT rank on unadjusted totals. If an adjustment cannot be applied to a player, say so and leave him in with the gap flagged. OUTPUT: 20-40 names, each with two lines - why he is here, and what the main doubt is. Then a section titled LIMITATIONS OF THIS LIST stating what data was missing, which player groups are underrepresented, and what would change the answer.
Bad - this returns names you already knew Who are the best available strikers under 25?
Why the bad one fails "Best" is undefined, "available" is undefined, there is no role, no league, no minutes threshold and no adjustment. An unconstrained question returns the most prominent names, because prominence is what an unconstrained ranking is measuring. You have paid for a search of four thousand players and been handed the forty you could have named yourself.
Homework

Try it yourself - before the next window ◐ 40-50 min total

  • Take last window's shortlist and mark each name: found by data, or brought by a scout who already knew the player. Count both columns. The ratio tells you what your process actually is, as opposed to what you say it is.
  • Pick one metric your recruitment team quotes regularly and ask for it league-adjusted and minutes-adjusted, with the before and after shown. Note whether the ranking moved. If it did not move at all, ask what was adjusted.
  • Write the brief format you will use next window on one side of one page: role as a job, constraints, required adjustments, minimum minutes, longlist with reasons, limitations section. Then name the human who owns each signing decision at your club, and if that takes more than five seconds, that is the finding.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · Your new recruitment model returns a top ten that your head of recruitment could have written from memory. What is the most likely explanation?

A model fed reputation, market value or club prestige will rank the famous players first, which is the exact opposite of the value recruitment analytics is meant to add. You paid for a search of four thousand players and got the forty you already knew, with decimal places attached. Ask what the features are before you widen anything.

2 · A striker in a weaker league has scored eighteen in 600 minutes and tops your list. What do you ask for first?

600 minutes is roughly seven matches, and a per-90 rate on that swings enormously. League level is not a small correction either. The same player can be first, third, second and fourth on four honest ways of counting, and until you have seen the adjusted version you do not know which player you are looking at.

3 · Your most experienced scout is furious that his preferred player is not on the data shortlist. What is the right move?

Both extremes destroy the process. A named exception with a stated rationale is healthy, and the reason the model missed him is usually a data gap, a role label or a minutes threshold, which is a finding worth more than the individual player. What you must never do is let the disagreement be settled by whoever brought a slide.
