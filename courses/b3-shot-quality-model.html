Building a shot-quality model

This is the session the whole track points at, and not because shot quality is the most important thing an analyst does. It is because a shot model is the cleanest place in sport to feel what a feature is actually worth, and the cleanest place to get caught overfitting. Tonight you build one feature at a time, watch the held-out number climb from 50% to 83% while the gap between fitted and held out stays at two points the whole way, and then add the one feature that makes the headline number jump eleven points and the real number fall six. Everything else in this course - opposition analysis, scouting, load management - is that same trap wearing different clothes.

Real world

What a serious model looks like, for scale. Commercial shot-quality models train on close to a million historical shots and integrate more than twenty variables including distance, angle, defensive pressure and goalkeeper location. You are not building that tonight and you do not need to - but knowing which of those variables your provider's number includes tells you exactly which of your team's chances it is systematically getting wrong.

Self-studyAccuracy is not the only thing to check3 min read▶

This simulator reports accuracy because it is the number everyone quotes and therefore the number that misleads everyone. In real work you would also want:

  • Calibration. Of all the chances your model called 0.20, did about 20% go in? A model can rank chances beautifully and still be systematically over-confident, which makes every aggregate you build on it wrong in the same direction.
  • Performance by segment. Overall accuracy hides a model that is excellent on central chances and useless on wide ones. Your team probably takes a distinctive mix of chances, so overall is the least relevant number to you.
  • Stability across seasons. The held-out number IS this, done once. Do it every season and you will catch a provider's definition change before it embarrasses you (b2).

Published research on shot models reports calibration alongside discrimination for exactly this reason. If a vendor will only show you one aggregate figure, that is information about the vendor.

Part 2 · the trap, and the honest nuance

When the number going up is the bad news 6 min live

Add shooter identity and reputation as a feature and the fitted score jumps to 96.0 - the largest single gain available anywhere on this page. The held-out score falls to 77.0, below where it was three rungs earlier. The gap goes from two points to nineteen. The model did not get better at judging chances; it memorised which players take them, and next August the squad changes.

LiveThe honest nuance: player effects are not the problem4 min▶

It would be easy and wrong to leave tonight believing that player information must never enter a shot model. The research says otherwise, and the distinction is the actual lesson:

  • What fails - raw shooter identity and reputation, unregularised, on a small sample. The model gets to memorise individuals, and with a few hundred shots per player it will. That is leakage wearing a jersey.
  • What works - Bayesian hierarchical models that POOL player effects with shrinkage, the Bayes-xG line of work. These do genuinely predict season goals for individual players and teams better than a plain overall xG model, because a player with 30 shots is pulled hard toward the population and only a player with a long record is allowed to move far from it.
  • The difference in one sentence - the naive version asks "who took it", the principled version asks "how much evidence do we have that this player differs from average, and how far should that move our estimate".

The reason to teach the failure first is that everyone builds the naive version by accident, and the metric rewards them for it. Once you have felt the gap open up, the hierarchical idea stops being a technique and becomes obvious.

Self-studyLeakage in the rest of your work3 min read▶

The same trap appears everywhere in a club, usually without a held-out set to catch it:

  • Opposition analysis - a pattern found in five matches will always look strong in those five matches. b6 handles this with counts, denominators and date ranges.
  • Recruitment - a model trained on players your club previously signed reproduces who your club previously signed, and there is no next season to test on until you have already spent the money. b7.
  • Injury risk - the most dangerous version, because the events are rare, the data is imbalanced, and a model that predicts nobody gets hurt looks accurate. b5, and the literature is explicit that these outputs are supportive information only.
  • Anything fitted on one season of tracking - which at Rivermouth is everything that uses pressure or keeper position. Be loud about it.

The habit generalises even when a clean held-out split is not available: ask what this model would have to memorise in order to look this good, and check whether it could.

Demo 2 of 2

Shot by shot, where the value actually sits ★ 10 min · explore

Aggregates hide the mechanism. Step through the eight shots with all five features on and read what each feature is worth on each individual chance - the split is different every time, which is precisely why a model beats a rule of thumb like "shots inside the box are good".

Shot 1, the tap-in: distance is 45% of the value, but the keeper being committed is another 25%. Two features you may not have.

Shot 2, the pressed header at 11 metres: body part and pressure together are half the value, and they cut it to 0.11 from what distance alone would say.

Shot 6, the 35-metre strike that went in: 0.03, and correct. Turn reputation on and watch it become 0.12 because a famous player hit it.

Shot 8, six metres out on the byline: 0.13. Angle is 45% of the value here and nothing else comes close.

Turn reputation on and step through all eight: famous shooters inflate, unknowns deflate, and not one estimate got closer to the truth.

Real world

Shot 6 is the row that costs analysts their credibility, and the row worth defending. A 3% chance went in. The striker is delighted, the crowd is delighted, and your model says it was a poor decision. Both are true: it was a poor decision that worked once, and over a season a team that takes those shots scores fewer goals than a team that does not. Your job is not to argue about the goal. It is to have said, before it happened, what kind of chance it was - and to have a count and a denominator ready when someone asks.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · Across the five real features the gap between fitted and held out stays at 2 points. What does a stable gap tell you?

A gap that stays flat while both numbers climb is the signature of genuine learning. When a feature starts widening the gap, it is teaching the model about the specific data it was fitted on - which is exactly what the sixth toggle does, taking the gap from 2 to 19.

2 · Is it always wrong to put player information into a shot model?

The distinction is the whole lesson. The naive version asks who took the shot and memorises individuals. The principled version asks how much evidence exists that this player differs from average, and pulls a player with 30 shots hard toward the population. The Bayes-xG work shows the second genuinely helps.

3 · A 35-metre strike your model rated 0.03 goes in. The striker wants it removed from the report. What do you do?

Option C is the anti-feature, offered as a peace treaty. The honest answer is that the goal was real and the chance was poor, both matter, and over a season a team taking those shots scores fewer goals. Have the count and the denominator ready before the conversation, not during it.
