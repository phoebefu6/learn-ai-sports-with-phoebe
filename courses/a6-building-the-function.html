Building the analytics function

Five sessions of what is possible, what is proven and what is sold. Tonight you decide what you are actually going to do, with the staff and the budget you have rather than the ones in the case study. The two hard truths of this session are unglamorous and load-bearing. First, sequencing beats ambition: a three-person department that ships one useful thing a quarter and earns one relationship a quarter will be trusted by January, and one that ships everything at once will be ignored by November. Second, analysis almost never dies at the modelling stage. It dies at the Communicate stage, in a room, in front of a coach who has already decided the meeting is over. You leave tonight with a four-quarter roadmap, a named relationship goal for each quarter, and a written standard your department will be held to.

Part 1 · capacity, honestly

Three people, one season 15 min live

Three analysts is roughly two people of delivery once you account for matchdays, travel, the requests that arrive at 9pm on a Friday, and the person who leaves in March. That is not pessimism, it is the planning number. A department that plans for three and delivers with two spends the whole season apologising, which is corrosive in a way that has nothing to do with the quality of the work. Plan for two, sequence ruthlessly, and say out loud what is not happening this year.

LiveWhy the sequence starts with opposition analysis5 min▶
  • Opposition analysis is where the evidence is strongest and the stakes are lowest. If your report on next week's opponent is a bit wrong, a coach adjusts and nobody is harmed. The feedback comes back within seven days, every single week, which is the fastest learning loop available anywhere in the club.
  • Shot quality is next because it is checkable. The public evidence base is deepest here. Commercial models train on close to a million historical shots across twenty-plus variables, and post-match expected goals predicts match outcome at around 0.656 accuracy - genuinely informative and clearly not oracular. Your version will be smaller and you should say so, but the ground is well mapped.
  • Recruitment comes third because the stakes rise. A shortlist affects someone's livelihood, and by this point your department has the standing to shortlist rather than decide.
  • Medical is last, always. Not because it matters least, but because it matters most and the models are weakest exactly there. Injury-prediction performance in the published literature runs from accuracy 52% and AUC 0.52 up to AUC 0.87, wildly inconsistent, with many studies landing at 0.63 to 0.65 which the literature itself calls poor. Walking into the medical room in month two with that is how a department gets written off for three years.

a4 and b5 carry the full treatment. The management point here is simply that these outputs are supportive information only, never a selection or return-to-play decision on their own, and your department should be the loudest voice in the building saying so.

LiveThe hiring question, answered in one sentence5 min▶

One person who can talk to coaches is worth more than two who cannot. That is the whole answer, and it is unpopular with everyone who has ever built a shortlist by counting technical skills.

  • The bottleneck is almost never modelling. The tools are commodity, the methods are documented, and the hard parts of the maths have been solved by people who published. What has not been solved is getting a coach to change something on a Tuesday.
  • Test for it in the interview, properly. Give the candidate a real finding and ninety seconds to explain it to someone who is not technical and is slightly impatient. You will learn more in that ninety seconds than in the rest of the process.
  • What that person actually needs technically is enough to know when a number is unsafe. Sample size, the difference between fitted and held out, and the instinct to check who is missing from the data. That is a smaller list than most job specs.
  • Do not hire three of the same person, and be wary of the candidate who wants to build the platform. One who lives in the coaching environment, one strong on data plumbing and one flexible pair of hands beats three modellers, and infrastructure work in year one is usually a way of avoiding the harder job of being useful this month.
Part 2 · the real failure mode

Adoption is a coaching problem, not a tooling problem 20 min live

Go back to the analysis loop from b1: Capture, Model, Judge, Communicate, Decide. Directors instinctively worry about stage 2, because that is where the technology is and where the vendors sell. In practice, work that gets ignored almost always died at stage 4. It was right, it was checked, it was defensible, and it was delivered as a PDF to somebody who was standing on a training pitch. The model was never the problem. The room was.

LiveThe one-slide rule, the room, and the move that beats both9 min▶

Three habits do most of the work here, and none of them is technical.

  • One slide. One claim. One recommended action. Not a summary slide in front of fourteen appendix slides - one slide, and the appendix exists only if someone asks. Choosing the single claim is what makes the analysis good, which is why the rule survives contact with reality.
  • Sit in the room. A PDF sent at 11pm is a document. The same finding delivered in ninety seconds while the coach is looking at you is a conversation, and only conversations change behaviour. If your analysts are not present when decisions get made, your department is a reporting service.
  • Put the uncertainty in the same sentence as the number. Not in a footnote. "We think it is about this, and here is what would change our mind" is a sentence a coach can act on.
  • Then the single most effective move available: be visibly right about something small, and visibly uncertain about something big. The small correct call is the deposit - something specific and checkable that resolves next Saturday in front of people, not a season projection. The visible uncertainty is what makes the deposit spendable, because everyone in that room has met an expert who was never unsure and has stopped believing them. Confidence without limits reads as sales; uncertainty without a track record reads as hedging. You need both, in that order.
  • Never let a bad call go unmentioned. Say it first, plainly, before anyone else raises it. A department that marks its own homework in public gets trusted with harder questions, and that is the mechanism behind the line from b1: a department is judged on what it refuses to claim.
LiveThe head coach who wants none of it6 min▶

Common, survivable, and every standard response makes it worse: mandating use produces compliance theatre, going over their head to the board produces an enemy, and waiting for the next coach wastes a year. b8 is the analyst session on all of this - send everyone.

  • Find out what the objection actually is. It is usually one of three things: a previous bad experience with a dashboard or a consultant, a fear of being second-guessed in public, or a straightforward view that the numbers do not describe the game they see. All three are answerable. None of them is answered by more numbers.
  • Solve a problem they already have, and make it something dull. Set-piece opponent tendencies. Turnaround time on clips. The thing their assistant currently does by hand until midnight.
  • Give it away, and protect them in public. No branding, no dashboard, no meeting invite - the staff should be able to use the work without acknowledging where it came from. And if a number contradicts a decision they made, it does not go to the board first. A department that leaks upward never gets shown anything again.
  • Know when to escalate. If the coach refuses a rail - selection, return to play or valuation being driven by a model, or an integrity rail from a5 - that is not an adoption problem and it goes to you and the board immediately.

The second one is shorter, names a specific and checkable thing, admits a limit, hands over something usable immediately, and ends with a question that lets the coach set the next piece of work. That last part is how you turn one interaction into a relationship.

Part 3 · the standards you enforce

Five things a director owns, not a team lead read and pick two

These are not technical matters delegated downward. Each one is a management standard, and each one fails silently unless somebody senior is holding it.

LiveProving value without overclaiming3 min▶
  • Claim decisions, not outcomes. "Recruitment used our shortlist for four of six signings" is verifiable. "We were worth six points" is not.
  • Count changed behaviour. How many times somebody did something different because of a piece of work. Small, honest, and it grows. Attributing points won to an analysis team damages you twice: nobody believes it, and it teaches the building that your numbers are marketing.
  • Report what you got wrong in the same document. A value report with no misses in it is a document nobody senior takes seriously.
  • An analytics department is judged on what it refuses to claim. That line is from b1 and it is the most useful sentence in this whole track.
LiveThe two-number discipline as a management standard3 min▶

This is the one people mistake for a technical detail, and it is why it needs a director enforcing it. The rule: never accept one accuracy number. Every claim arrives with what the model scored on the data it was fitted to and what it scored on data it had never seen, and you watch the gap.

  • A small gap means it learned the game. A large gap means it learned this specific squad and this specific season, and next August it quietly stops working without telling anyone.
  • The failure mode looks exactly like success. The flattering number goes up first. That is what makes this dangerous, and it is why the check has to be a standing requirement rather than a good habit.
  • Write it into how work is presented. Two numbers and a gap on every slide that contains a model. No exceptions for vendors, and no exceptions for the commercial side either.
  • Ask vendors for both numbers. A supplier with only one has told you something important. b1 and b3 demonstrate this live with a shot model where one bad feature makes the headline number climb and the real one fall - if your analysts have not seen it, that is a 20-minute fix.
LiveWhen to buy and when to build3 min▶

A short rule that survives most cases: buy what every club needs identically, build what is specific to how your club plays.

  • Buy event and tracking data, video infrastructure, wearable platforms, and anything where being different from other clubs has no value.
  • Build the weekly opposition report, the shortlisting logic that reflects your recruitment philosophy, and any analysis tied to your own playing model. Nobody will sell you these because they are not products.
  • Never buy a black box that touches the decision line. If you cannot see the features, you cannot defend the output, and a5 and a4 both make that your problem rather than the vendor's.
LiveWhen the model disagrees with the coach3 min▶
  • The coach is often right, and the honest reason is stage 3 of the loop: Judge. A model has no context, no squad, no dressing room and no knowledge that a player's father is ill this week. Analysts own the sample size; coaches own a category of information that never reaches the data.
  • Disagreement is information, so treat it that way. Ask what the model is missing. The answer is frequently a feature nobody had thought to capture, and that conversation is worth more than winning the argument.
  • The decision line does not move. Selection, return to play and valuation are decided by named humans with the model in the room. Disagreement changes who is persuaded, not who decides.
Self-studyThe career shape of an analyst, and why they leave4 min read▶
  • The ceiling is visible from day one. In a three-person department there is one senior role and it is occupied. Say this honestly at hire and talk about what the person will be able to do next, wherever they do it.
  • Being ignored is what actually burns people out, not the workload - and the workload is real, because matchdays and travel are relentless and the recovery time is never scheduled. An analyst who is listened to will work absurd hours happily. One who is not will leave regardless of what you pay them, which is why Part 2 of this session is a retention strategy as much as an adoption one.
  • Plan for the departure you did not want. Written handovers, shared code, nothing living only on one laptop. In a three-person team, one unplanned exit removes a third of the department and, if you were careless, most of its memory.
Build-along · in session

Write your own four quarters ★ 7 min · everyone writes

One card, four rows. One deliverable per quarter and one named relationship goal per quarter - a person, not a department. If you cannot name the person, you have not decided anything yet.

Q1 · ships: the one thing that lands every week without fail. Write the deliverable and the day it appears. Builds trust with: name the person. Usually the head coach or their assistant.

Q2 · ships: the first thing that carries two numbers on every claim. Builds trust with: name the person, and write what they will ask you for once they trust you.

Q3 · ships: the first output that touches someone's livelihood, so the department shortlists and people decide. Builds trust with: name the person in recruitment or the sporting director.

Q4 · ships: the availability picture, exposure and prior injury only, supportive information and nothing more. Builds trust with: name the clinician. This is the relationship the whole year was building toward.

Now the two lines underneath. First: what is not happening this year - say it to the board before they ask, because the requests you decline in October are the ones that would have killed the roadmap in February. Second, the written standard: two numbers and a gap on every slide that contains a model, vendors and the commercial side included. Sign it and circulate it.

The test for a finished roadmap Read it to somebody who does not work in analytics and ask them what your department will have done by Christmas. If they can answer in one sentence, it is a plan. If they cannot, it is a wish list with dates on it.
Homework

Try it yourself - this month ◐ 45-70 min total

  • Finish the roadmap - four quarters, one deliverable and one named relationship each, plus the line saying what is not happening this year. Take it to whoever signs off your headcount.
  • Name the one question your head coach actually asks every week, in their words rather than yours. Then check honestly whether your department answers it. If it does not, that is your Q1 deliverable and everything else moves.
  • Set the two-number rule as a written standard. One paragraph, circulated, applied to internal work and vendor claims alike. Then ask your current suppliers for both numbers and see who can produce them.
  • Book yourself into one opposition meeting this month and watch how the work lands in the room. Do not present anything. Just watch stage 4 happen.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · Your new three-person department wants to start with an injury-risk model, because that is what the board keeps asking about. What do you tell them?

Medical matters most and is exactly where the models are weakest: published injury-prediction performance runs from accuracy 52% and AUC 0.52 up to AUC 0.87 and is inconsistent across studies, with many landing at 0.63 to 0.65. Walking into a sceptical medical room in month two with that is how a department gets written off for three years. Opposition analysis gives you a seven-day feedback loop at almost no risk, and it earns the standing you will need later.

2 · Your department is technically strong, the work is correct, and the coaching staff ignore all of it. Where has it most likely failed?

Analysis is ignored at Communicate far more often than it fails at Model. Department A shipped fourteen correct dashboards nobody asked for and was opened twice. Department B answered the question the coach was already asking out loud, in one page, in the room, and by season end was trusted with selection conversations. Same people, same data, same season.

3 · You have budget for one more analyst. Two candidates: a stronger modeller who is uncomfortable presenting, and a decent modeller who explains a finding to a sceptical coach in ninety seconds. Who do you hire?

The modelling is commodity and the methods are documented. What is not solved is getting a coach to change something on a Tuesday. The technical bar that actually matters is knowing when a number is unsafe - sample size, fitted versus held out, and who is missing from the data - and that is a shorter list than most job specs assume. Holding the budget for a unicorn costs you a season.
