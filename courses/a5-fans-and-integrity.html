Fans, commercial and integrity

Everything so far has been about the pitch, the medical room and the transfer market, where models are weak and the stakes are a person's body or livelihood. Tonight moves to the other end of the club, where the data is cleaner, the feedback is faster and the payback is real. Then it moves to the part nobody puts on a slide: the moment your club produces a predictive output about its own matches, that output stops being a piece of analysis and becomes a piece of information with a market value to people betting on the fixture. This session gives you the commercial priorities a three-person department can actually deliver, and the five rails that keep the rest of it safe. Wagering strategy is out of scope here and everywhere in this course. The rails exist to protect the competition and the people in it.

Part 1 · the fast end of the club

The lowest-stakes, fastest-moving part of the club 15 min live

Ticketing, pricing, content, membership and matchday operations share three properties that performance and medical work do not: the data is generated by your own systems rather than a provider, the outcome is visible within days rather than a season, and nobody's body or career is on the line. That combination is why this is where a small analytics department can prove itself fastest. It is also why the risk here has a different shape. When a commercial model goes wrong, the damage is not safety, it is fairness - a fan who concludes the club treated them differently from the person sitting next to them.

LiveThe two that actually pay a mid-table club5 min▶

If you take one thing from this half of the session, take this: the biggest realistic commercial win at a club your size is demand forecasting and membership churn, and it is not a chatbot. The chatbot is the thing your commercial director will bring back from a conference. It is visible, it demos well, and at a mid-table club it usually replaces a form nobody was struggling with.

  • Demand forecasting. How many people turn up for a Tuesday night fixture in February against a side in fourteenth. You already have the history, the answer is checkable within a week, and it drives staffing, catering, stewarding and allocation decisions that all cost real money today.
  • Membership churn. Which season-ticket holders are drifting, and which of them respond to a phone call from a human rather than an email. This is the one commercial use case where a model output leads directly to a named person doing something specific, which is why it works.
  • Matchday operations. Queue times, gate flow, kiosk stock. Small, unglamorous, low risk, and it improves the thing fans actually complain about.
  • Personalised content and highlights. Genuinely useful and increasingly cheap to produce. The line to watch is the point where personalisation stops feeling like service and starts feeling like surveillance, and that line is set by fans, not by you.

Notice what all four have in common. The decision at the end is commercial and reversible. Nobody's fitness, contract or medical record is involved. That is precisely why this is a good place for a small department to build a track record before it goes anywhere near the medical room.

LiveDynamic pricing, and where fans find it unacceptable5 min▶

Dynamic pricing sits in the top right of the picture for a reason. It has the fastest revenue payback of anything on the board and the highest chance of a reputational incident, and those two facts are the same fact: it works by charging different people different amounts for the same seat.

  • Fans accept variation they can see the logic of. A bigger fixture costs more. An early booking costs less. A category of seat costs what it costs. All of this has been true for decades and nobody objects.
  • Fans reject variation that feels personal. The moment the price appears to depend on who is looking rather than what is being sold, the conversation stops being about pricing and becomes about whether the club respects them. That is a hard conversation to win, because they are not wrong about the mechanism.
  • The rule that survives contact with a fan forum: price the fixture and the seat, never the person. If you cannot explain a price difference in one sentence to a supporter on local radio, do not ship it.
  • Loyalty must never be punished by the model. The season-ticket holder who buys every away ticket is the most predictable buyer you have, which means a naive optimiser will quietly charge them more. Check for this explicitly, because it will happen by default and nobody will notice until it is a headline.
Real world

The pattern from b1 turns up here too. A pricing model tuned hard on last season's data will look excellent on last season's data. The two-number discipline is not a technical nicety confined to the analyst track - fit on one season, test on a season the model has never seen, and report both numbers to the commercial board. A pricing model with a big gap between the two has learned last year's fixture list, not your fanbase.

Self-studyWhat to ask before you buy any commercial AI product3 min read▶

Most of what is sold into the commercial side of a club is a product, not a project. Five questions separate the ones worth buying:

  • What decision changes because of this? If nobody in the building does anything different on a Monday morning, it is a dashboard with a subscription fee.
  • Whose data leaves the club, and where does it go? Fan data is personal data. a2 mapped the estate and a4 covered the rights; the answer belongs in writing before signature, not after.
  • What are the two numbers? Ask for performance on data the vendor fitted on and on data they did not. A vendor who only has one number has told you something.
  • What does it do when it is wrong? A churn model that is wrong costs you a phone call. A pricing model that is wrong costs you a supporters' trust meeting. Size the failure, not the success.
  • Can we turn it off? Ask how the contract ends and who keeps the data when it does.
Part 2 · the part nobody puts on a slide

The integrity rails 22 min live

Here is the sentence that changes how you think about your own analytics department. The moment your club produces a predictive output about its own matches, that output becomes sensitive in a way that has nothing to do with commerce. Team news, injury status, expected line-ups, a fitness flag on a first-choice centre back, an internal probability that a striker starts - all of it has value to people betting on the match. Your analysts did not create that value and they cannot remove it. What you can do is control who holds it and when it moves.

LiveThe five rails, in board language8 min▶

These are short on purpose. If a rail does not fit in a sentence, staff will not follow it and the club cannot enforce it.

  • 1 · Inside information stays inside, and it is defined in writing. The definition is the whole exercise. Most clubs have a vague shared understanding that team news is confidential and nothing at all about internal probabilities, fitness flags, training-ground observations or the contents of a load report. Write the list. Circulate the list. Review it once a season.
  • 2 · No predictive output about your own fixtures is published or shared ahead of official team news. Not to a partner, not to a broadcaster, not in a sponsor deck, not in a slide that leaves the building. After official team news, the calculus changes and you can decide commercially. Before it, the answer is no.
  • 3 · Access is logged. Who can see the availability picture, the internal line-up model, the load flags. A list of names, reviewed when people join and when they leave. The log matters more than the lock, because the question you will one day have to answer is not "was it secure" but "who had it".
  • 4 · Staff and players get clear rules about what they may say and to whom. Written, brief, repeated at the start of each season, and covering the informal channels where this actually leaks - a group chat, an agent, a family member, a former teammate who now works in media.
  • 5 · Anything that touches a betting partner has a documented wall. Many clubs have commercial relationships in this space. That is a business decision above your analytics department. What is not optional is a written separation with a named owner, showing exactly what data flows, what does not, and who checks.

Two of those five cost nothing but an afternoon of writing. The other three cost a review meeting a year. This is the cheapest risk control in the entire course, and it is the one most likely to be missing at your club right now.

LiveWhy an internal probability is different from an opinion6 min▶

The objection you will hear is that everybody speculates about line-ups, so an internal model is no different. It is different, and the difference is worth being able to explain calmly.

  • It is specific. A pundit's guess and a number produced from your own training-ground data are not the same object, even when they happen to agree. One is inference from the outside; the other is a measurement from the inside.
  • It is attributable. If it moves, people can tell where it came from. A club is a small place and analytics outputs have fingerprints.
  • It is early. Value in this context is almost entirely about timing. The same information released at the official moment is worth nothing to anyone with an unfair intent, and released two hours earlier to one party it is worth a great deal.
  • It travels. A screenshot of a probability leaves the building faster than a sentence, is trusted more because it looks like a fact, and cannot be walked back.

The practical instruction to your department is one line: internal probabilities about your own fixtures are treated exactly like team news, and released at the same moment or not at all. The analysts will not find this controversial. The people who find it inconvenient are usually in commercial or comms, which is why this session exists.

Say the scope out loud in the room When you brief this to staff, say plainly that the club does not teach, model or discuss betting strategy, and that these rails are not about markets at all. They are about protecting the competition and protecting the people in it, including your own players, who are the ones exposed when information about their fitness moves in the wrong way.
Self-studyThe medical gate is stricter, and a4 explains why3 min read▶

Gate 3 in the picture is different from the other two. Gates 1 and 2 are about timing - the same information becomes shareable at a defined moment. Gate 3 is not a timing question at all.

  • Availability is a squad fact. Diagnosis is a medical record. The coach needs to know who is available. The coach does not need the injury detail, and the board certainly does not.
  • Risk scores are not a workaround. Turning a medical fact into a model output does not change what it is. A5 does not relax anything a4 established.
  • The decision line from b1 still holds. Return to play is a clinician's decision. Nothing on this page moves it, and nothing commercial ever should.
  • This is orientation, not medical or legal advice. The specifics of what may be disclosed about a player's health belong with clinicians and counsel at your club.
Part 3 · the conversations this creates

Five conversations you will have this season read and pick two

Each of these is a real meeting that follows from the two halves above. Pick the two most likely at your club and read those properly.

LiveFan-facing analytics that inform rather than mislead3 min▶

Publishing a probability to fans is a communication problem before it is an analytics problem. The failure mode is not inaccuracy, it is a viewer who reads a probability as a verdict.

  • A probability is not a scoreline that should have happened. A chance of 0.03 that goes in was still a poor chance, and the goal was still real. Both facts are useful; a graphic that implies only one of them is misleading even when the number is right.
  • Never publish a number your own department would not act on. If your analysts would want a bigger sample before believing it, your fans deserve the same caveat.
  • Uncertainty belongs in the same sentence as the number, in fan content exactly as in a coach briefing.

b9 is the full session on fan-facing and broadcast analytics, including how to explain a probability on air. Send whoever runs your content team to it.

LiveWhen a sponsor wants access to your data3 min▶

This arrives inside a renewal conversation, framed as an activation, and it is usually the first time anyone asks what your club actually holds. Three questions, in order:

  • What exactly flows, and is any of it predictive about our own fixtures? If yes, gate 2 applies and the answer is not before official team news.
  • Is any party to this agreement connected to a betting interest? If yes, rail 5 applies and you need a documented wall with a named owner before anything moves.
  • Is any of it personal data about fans or players? If yes, a2 and a4 govern it, and the lawful basis has to exist before the deal, not after.

The commercial answer can still be yes. It just cannot be an undocumented yes.

LiveWhat to publish about your own performance, and what to hold3 min▶

Clubs increasingly publish their own analysis, and it is good content. The dividing line is simple once you see it.

  • Retrospective is generally fine. What happened, described honestly, after the fact.
  • Prospective about your own fixtures is not, until official team news has gone out. This includes anything that lets a reader infer availability.
  • Be careful with the accidental leak. A season-long minutes chart published on a Thursday can reveal a fitness situation nobody meant to disclose. Have one person check outbound analytics content against the gates.
  • Publishing your own weaknesses builds more credibility than publishing your strengths, and it is the cheapest trust you will ever buy.
LivePlayers, image rights and AI-generated content3 min▶

Generated content involving players is now cheap enough that somebody in your building is already experimenting. Get ahead of it with four positions:

  • A player's likeness is theirs, and the agreement predates the technology. Check whether existing image-rights terms contemplate synthetic content at all. Usually they do not, which means silence is not consent.
  • Voice is likeness too, and it is the one people forget until it is a problem.
  • Label generated content as generated. If a fan cannot tell, you have created a small credibility debt that becomes expensive the first time something goes wrong.
  • Give players a veto that is easy to use. A route that requires an agent and a lawyer is not a veto.

This connects directly to a4. A player's data and a player's likeness raise the same underlying question: the club holds something that belongs to a person, and the person should be able to see and object to how it is used.

Self-studyThe club that gets fan trust right4 min read▶

Something is shifting in what fans expect, and it is worth naming before it arrives at your club as a complaint rather than an opportunity.

  • Fans increasingly want to know what you hold on them. Not in a hostile way. They want to know whether the price they saw was the price everyone saw, whether the email they got was targeted, and what happens to their data when they stop being a member.
  • Transparency is becoming a differentiator rather than a compliance chore. A club that can say clearly what it collects, why, and how to opt out is doing something most of its competitors cannot, and it costs a page of writing.
  • The trust runs in both directions. Fans who understand what you hold are more willing to share more, which is the practical commercial argument for doing this properly.
  • Say it in fan language, not policy language. One page, plain sentences, on the same site where they buy tickets. If it needs a lawyer to read, it is not transparency.
  • Test it the honest way. Read your own fan data page to a supporter and watch their face. That is your entire user research budget and it is enough.
Homework

Before session 6 ◐ 40-60 min total

  • List every predictive output your club produces - models, projections, availability pictures, internal line-up probabilities, load flags, anything with a number attached to a future event. Then mark which of them could move a betting market if it left the building early. Most directors are surprised by the length of the second list.
  • Write your inside-information definition, in under 200 words, in language a first-team player and a commercial intern would both understand. If it is longer than that, it will not be read.
  • Check whether any partner has data access nobody documented. Ask your commercial lead, your IT lead and your data provider separately, then compare the three answers. The gap between them is the finding.
  • Rank your commercial use cases on the two axes from Part 1 with your own commercial director in the room, and agree the top two for next season.
  • Find out who at your club would sign off publishing a piece of analysis about an upcoming fixture. If the answer is "nobody in particular", that is rail 2 failing quietly.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · Your analysts have an internal model that puts a first-choice striker at 30% likely to start Saturday. A journalist asks on Thursday. What do you do?

Rail 2. A predictive output about your own fixture is not published or shared ahead of official team news, to anyone. The value of that information is almost entirely about timing, and a number produced from your own training-ground data is a measurement from the inside, not a pundit's guess. Releasing it to one party early is the exact thing the rail exists to prevent.

2 · Your commercial director comes back from a conference wanting a fan chatbot. Where is the fastest realistic payback for a mid-table club with three analysts?

The chatbot is the most requested and the least valuable at your size. Dynamic pricing does pay back fast, but it sits in the high-risk corner because it works by charging different people different amounts, and that is where fans conclude the club treated them unfairly. Demand forecasting and churn use data you already own, produce a checkable answer within a week, and lead to a named person doing something specific.

3 · A sponsor asks for a feed of your performance data as part of a renewal. What is the first question?

The commercial answer can still be yes. What it cannot be is an undocumented yes. Gate 2 governs anything predictive about your own fixtures, rail 5 governs anything touching a betting interest, and a2 and a4 govern anything that is personal data about fans or players. Anonymisation is a technique, not an answer to the first question.
