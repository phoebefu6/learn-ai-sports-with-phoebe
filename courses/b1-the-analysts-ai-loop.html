The sports analyst's AI loop

Sport got modelled before almost anything else, which means it has had longer than most fields to learn where models earn their place and where they quietly do damage. Commercial shot-quality models now train on close to a million historical shots across twenty-plus variables, and post-match expected goals predicts match outcome at around 0.656 accuracy - genuinely useful, and nowhere near the certainty the graphic implies. Meanwhile injury-risk models in the published literature range from accuracy 52% and AUC 0.52 up to AUC 0.87, wildly inconsistent, and the reviews are blunt that these outputs are supportive information only and must never decide selection or return to play on their own. Tonight you get the frame: the analysis loop, the decision line no model crosses, and a live shot model where switching on the wrong feature makes the headline number go UP.

Part 1 · the spine of the track

The analysis loop 7 min live

Every piece of analysis you produce runs the same five stages: Capture (what happened, as data), Model (what does it imply), Judge (is that true here, for this squad, this season), Communicate (say it so a coach can act), Decide (someone commits, and owns it). Machines are now very good at two of those, adequate at a third, and have no business at all in the last one.

LiveThe two-number discipline4 min▶

One habit prevents most of the damage an analytics department does to its own credibility, and it takes ten seconds: never report one accuracy number. Report two - how the model does on the data it was fitted to, and how it does on data it has never seen. In sport the natural split is brutal and useful: fit on this season, test on next.

  • A small gap - a couple of points - means the model has learned something about the game.
  • A large gap means it has learned something about this specific squad, this specific season, or worse, this specific set of players. Next August the squad changes and the model quietly stops working, usually without telling anyone.
  • The number that flatters you goes up first. That is what makes this dangerous: the failure mode looks exactly like success on the metric most people report.

Tonight's demo is built to make you feel this. There is a sixth toggle on the shot model, and when you switch it on the headline number climbs eleven points. It is the worst thing you can do to the model.

Real world

Scale gives you an idea of what a serious model looks like. Commercial shot-quality models train on close to a million historical shots and use twenty-plus variables including distance, angle, defensive pressure and goalkeeper position. Your club has one season of tracking data. That is not a reason to do nothing - it is a reason to be extremely disciplined about the held-out number, because with a small sample the fitted number will tell you whatever you want to hear.

Self-studyWhat xG actually is, and what it is not3 min read▶

Expected goals is a probability attached to a chance: given everything we know about shots like this one, how often does it end up in the net? Four things follow that people constantly get wrong:

  • It is not a verdict on the finish. A 0.03 chance that goes in was still a 0.03 chance. The goal was real; the chance was poor. Both are true and the second one is the useful one.
  • It is not a prediction of the match. Post-match xG predicted match outcome at around 0.656 accuracy in a peer-reviewed Bundesliga comparison - clearly informative, clearly not oracular, and worth quoting the next time someone treats an xG graphic as a scoreline that should have happened.
  • It is a sample-size instrument. Over one match it is noisy. Over a season it is one of the better things you have.
  • It is a model, so it inherits its features. A model without defensive pressure will systematically overrate crowded chances. Knowing which features your provider uses is not trivia, it is the difference between reading the number and being read by it.
Part 2 · the constraint that shapes the whole course

The decision line 6 min live

Three decisions in a club affect a person's body, livelihood or career, and the published evidence is clear that models are not good enough to make any of them alone. This is not caution for its own sake. The injury-prediction literature is the sharpest example anywhere in applied machine learning of a field where the stakes and the model quality point in opposite directions.

LiveWhy injury models underperform, in plain terms4 min▶

This is worth understanding properly, because you will be asked for an injury model within your first year and the way you answer determines whether the medical department ever trusts you.

  • Injuries are rare. Rare events make imbalanced datasets, and imbalanced datasets push a model toward the safe, useless prediction that nobody gets hurt. It will look accurate.
  • The published performance is genuinely inconsistent. The systematic review range runs from accuracy 52% and AUC 0.52 - which is close to a coin toss - up to AUC 0.87, with a lot of studies landing at 0.63 to 0.65, which the literature itself describes as poor.
  • Generalisability is weak. Results move with environment, team context and how things were measured, so a model that worked at another club is a hypothesis at yours, not a tool.
  • GPS workload is context dependent. It captures short-term exposure well and is not the whole story of why a hamstring goes.
  • The strongest signal is unglamorous. Prior injury, with hazard and odds ratios often above 2.0. Any model that does not beat "who got injured before" is not earning its place.

The recommendation in the literature is explicit: these outputs are supportive information, to be integrated with clinical judgment, athlete history, wellness data, training context and staff expertise - and never used in isolation for selection or return-to-play decisions. b5 is the whole session on this, and it is not medical advice; it is how to be useful next to people who give medical advice.

Self-studyHow to say no to a request you cannot deliver2 min read▶

A director will eventually ask for something the data cannot support - who will get injured, who to sell, whether a player is finished. The useful response is never a flat no, and never a model you do not believe in:

  • Name the decision behind the request. "Who will get injured" is usually really "how do I plan the squad for a congested December". That second question has an answerable version.
  • Offer the honest version. Exposure, prior injury, minutes load and availability history give a genuinely useful planning picture without pretending to predict an event.
  • Say what would change your answer. More seasons, better medical records, a shared definition of what counts as an injury.
  • Put the uncertainty in the same sentence as the number, every time. b8 is entirely about this.

An analytics department is trusted or ignored on the basis of what it refuses to claim, not on the sophistication of what it builds.

Build the shot model, feature by feature ★ 12 min · everyone runs it

Two numbers, always: this season is what the model scores on the data it was fitted to, next season is what it scores on data it has never seen. Add features one at a time and watch both. The ladder is 50 → 64 → 71 → 75 → 80 → 83 on the number that matters, with the gap holding steady at two points the whole way up. Then turn on the sixth toggle.

No features: 52.0 / 50.0. The model predicts the league average for every shot. Honest, and useless.

+ Distance: 66.0 / 64.0. The biggest single jump, as in every published shot model.

+ Angle: 73.0 / 71.0. Look at the 6-metre chance on the byline - distance alone called it a huge chance and it is 0.13.

+ Body part: 77.0 / 75.0. + Defender pressure: 82.0 / 80.0. Pressure needs tracking data, not event data (b2).

+ Keeper position: 85.0 / 83.0. Now find the 35-metre strike that went in. The model still says 0.03, and the model is right.

Now switch on ⚠ Shooter reputation. This season jumps to 96.0. Read the second number before you celebrate.

What just happened Next season fell to 77.0 and the gap went from 2 points to 19. The model did not get better at judging chances; it learned which players take them, and next August the squad changes. If you had reported one number - the one that went up - you would have shipped it.
Demo 2 of 2

One shot at a time ★ 10 min · explore

The aggregate hides the interesting part. Step through the eight shots and watch what each feature is worth on each one - the numbers are different every time, which is the entire reason a model beats a rule of thumb.

Shot 1, the tap-in: distance does most of the work, but the keeper being committed is a quarter of the value on its own.

Shot 3, 18 metres at a tight angle: angle is the largest term. Turn reputation on and watch this one get worse.

Shot 6, the 35-metre strike that went in: with all five features the model says 0.03 and it is correct. A goal does not make a good chance.

Shot 8, six metres out on the byline: 0.13. Say out loud why, then imagine explaining it to a striker who is furious about it.

Turn reputation on and step through all eight. The famous players' shots inflate, the unknown players' deflate, and nothing got more accurate.

Real world

The honest nuance, because the anti-lever is not the whole story. Player effects are not inherently wrong. Bayesian hierarchical approaches that pool player effects with shrinkage - the Bayes-xG line of work - do genuinely improve season-level prediction for individual players and teams over a plain xG model. What the toggle demonstrates is the naive version: raw identity and reputation, unregularised, on a small sample. The difference between those two is most of what separates a working analytics department from one that produces impressive numbers nobody can rely on.

Homework

Try it yourself - this week ◐ 25-35 min total

  • Run the full ladder until you can predict which shots move at each rung. Then run the reputation toggle and write one sentence about what happened to each number.
  • Find out which features your own xG provider uses. If defensive pressure is not among them, note which of your team's chances are being systematically overrated.
  • Take one number your department reported last month and produce the second number for it. If you cannot, that is the finding.
  • Write the decision line for your club on one card - selection, return to play, valuation - and check whether any current dashboard crosses it.
  • Ask your medical lead what would make them trust an analytics output. Do not argue. Write it down.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · You switch on shooter reputation and this season's accuracy jumps from 85.0 to 96.0. What should you do?

The gap went from 2 points to 19. The model learned who takes the shots rather than what makes a shot good, and next August the squad changes. This is exactly why you never report one number - the failure mode looks like success on the metric most people quote.

2 · A 35-metre strike with a model value of 0.03 goes in. What does that tell you about the model?

A probability is not a prediction of one event. The goal was real and the chance was still poor, and both facts are useful. Adjusting a model because a low-probability event happened is how you destroy it one anecdote at a time.

3 · A director asks for a model that says who will get injured next month. What is the honest response?

Both extremes are wrong. Published performance is real but inconsistent, from AUC 0.52 to 0.87 with many studies at 0.63-0.65, injuries are rare enough to make the data badly imbalanced, and the literature is explicit that outputs are supportive only. The useful move is to answer the planning question that was actually being asked.
