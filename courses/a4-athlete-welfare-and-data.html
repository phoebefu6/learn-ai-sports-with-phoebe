Athlete welfare and data rights

Read this before anything else on the page. This session is orientation for conversations you need to have with your medical lead, your club's counsel and player representatives. It is not medical advice and it is not legal advice, and nothing here states a rule as settled for your jurisdiction, your league or your competition. What it does is give you enough of the published evidence and enough of the structure to walk into those conversations able to ask a good question rather than repeat a vendor's claim. The people who decide are your clinicians and your lawyers. Your job tonight is to know what to ask them.

Part 0

What this session is, and what it is not

Same club as every session: Rivermouth FC - mid-table, three people in analytics, six seasons of event data, tracking for this season only, GPS on every player, and a medical department that has been burned by a dashboard before and does not trust analytics yet. That last detail stops being background tonight and becomes the subject. a3 put a model next to a player's livelihood. a4 puts one next to a player's body, which is where the stakes are highest and, uncomfortably, where the published model quality is weakest.

Orientation only, and it stays that way for the whole session Nothing on this page is medical advice or legal advice. Clinical decisions belong to your clinicians. Data-protection obligations differ by jurisdiction, league and competition, and yours must come from your club's counsel and your player representatives, not from a course page. Where this session sounds definite, read it as "here is the question to take to them".

Part 1 · before you sign anything

The evidence you must know before you buy an injury model 17 min live

You will be pitched an injury-prediction product. It will be well presented and the demo will be convincing. The published evidence base is more sobering than the pitch, and knowing it is the difference between a purchase your medical department can live with and one that costs you their trust permanently. Everything in this part is a summary of published research, offered as orientation. It is not medical advice, and it is not a judgement on any specific product or any specific player.

LiveSix things the literature says, in plain language8 min▶

Take these to your medical lead rather than to a vendor. Every one of them is a summary of published research and none of it is medical advice or a rule for your club.

  • The performance range is wide and inconsistent. Published injury-prediction models run from accuracy 52% and AUC 0.52, which is close to a coin toss, up to AUC 0.87. The spread is not noise around a good answer; it is genuine inconsistency across studies, and many land at AUC 0.63 to 0.65, which the literature itself describes as poor.
  • Injuries are rare, and rarity distorts everything. Low injury incidence produces imbalanced datasets, which bias predictions toward the non-injured majority. A model can drift toward effectively predicting that nobody gets hurt and still report a flattering accuracy figure. This is why one headline number should never be enough for you.
  • Generalisability is weak. Results move with environment, team context and how things were assessed. A model that performed well at another club, in another league, with another medical team's definitions, is a hypothesis at your club rather than a tool you have acquired.
  • GPS workload is real but partial. Wearable-derived workload variables capture short-term exposure and are context dependent. They are one input among several, and relatively few studies exploit the full granularity of tracking data.
  • The strongest single predictor is unglamorous. Prior injury, with hazard and odds ratios often above 2.0. Any product that cannot beat a careful reading of a player's own history has not earned a place in your budget.
  • The standing recommendation is explicit. Outputs should be used only as supportive information, integrated with clinical judgment, athlete history, wellness data, training context and staff expertise - and never in isolation for selection or return-to-play decisions.
The question that separates a serious vendor from a confident one "What is the held-out performance on a squad the model has never seen, in a league it was not trained on, using our definition of an injury?" A serious supplier will give you a number and a caveat. A confident one will change the subject to the interface. Whatever you are told, the decision it informs still belongs to your clinician.
LiveHow to buy this without losing your medical department5 min▶
  • Let the medical lead set the question. If the first question comes from the commercial side, the tool arrives as something being done to the medical department rather than for it.
  • Write the supportive-only rule into the contract and the workflow, not just into the slide deck. It is worth almost nothing as a stated intention and quite a lot as a documented rail.
  • Run it silently first. Let it produce outputs nobody acts on for a period your clinicians choose, and review what it would have said. This is cheap, and it converts an argument into evidence.

None of the above is medical or legal advice. It is a purchasing posture, and the specific terms belong to your counsel.

Part 2 · the part that outlives the technology

Whose data is it 15 min live

Wearable and medical data is about a person's body and their livelihood at the same time, which is what makes it different from every other dataset in your club. A shot map is about a match. A hamstring record is about a man who has a mortgage. Everything below is orientation for the conversation you need to have with your counsel, your medical lead and player representatives. It is not legal advice, and the rules that bind you depend on your jurisdiction, your league and your competition.

LiveFive practical rails, and the conversation each one starts7 min▶

These are working practices that clubs commonly adopt. They are not a compliance checklist and they are not legal advice. Treat each one as the opening of a conversation with your counsel and your medical lead.

LiveThe power imbalance, said honestly5 min▶

A player asked to consent by their employer is not consenting freely in the ordinary sense of the word. A 19-year-old on a first professional contract, asked by the club that controls his career whether he agrees to wear a device that tracks him, is not in a position to weigh that request the way a consumer weighs a cookie banner. He may genuinely be happy to. He also may not feel able to say no, and from the outside those two look identical.

  • This is why player representation belongs in the conversation early, not after a policy has been drafted and is being presented. Union representatives, the players' own representatives, and a senior player who will tell you the truth.
  • Consent is not the only possible basis, and it may not be the right one. Which basis applies to which purpose at your club is a question for your counsel, and the answer varies by jurisdiction.
  • Design as if consent could be withdrawn tomorrow. If withdrawing would break your entire operation, you have built something that depends on people not exercising a right.

None of this is legal advice. It is the reason to get legal advice, and to get it with player representatives in the room rather than after them.

Build-along, five minutes, on paper. Write the four access levels for your own club. Not the ideal ones. The real ones, as they operate today, including the informal access nobody has ever written down.

Level 1, clinician. Name the people, not the roles. Who at your club can see a diagnosis today, and who granted that access?

Level 2, medical support. Rehab and conditioning staff. What do they see, and is it what the role needs or what the system happens to show them?

Level 3, analyst. Write the sentence that defines the limit. "Availability and exposure, never diagnosis" is a good start. Then check the tool enforces it.

Level 4, recruitment. Including scouts and anyone in a transfer conversation. Draw the wall above this line and be honest about where it currently leaks.

The crossing. Write what a documented, case-by-case crossing looks like at your club, and who signs it. Take the page to your counsel, not to a vendor.

Good - availability and exposure, no clinical detail Summarise squad AVAILABILITY and EXPOSURE for the coming week. For each player report only: available / unavailable / limited (as recorded by the medical team), minutes played in each of the last four matches, training sessions completed, and days since last full session. HARD CONSTRAINTS - follow all of these: - Do NOT output any diagnosis, injury type, body part, imaging result, treatment, prognosis or expected return date. - Do NOT produce a return-to-play recommendation, a readiness score, or any statement about whether a player should or should not play. - Do NOT rank players by risk. - If a field you were given contains clinical detail, exclude it and say that you excluded it rather than summarising it. Close with this line: "This is availability information only. Selection and return-to-play decisions are made by the coaching and medical staff."
Bad - do not send this, and note why Which players are most likely to get injured next month, and should they play?
Why the bad one fails, on three separate counts It asks for a prediction the published evidence does not support at the confidence the question implies. It asks a model to answer a selection question, which sits on the decision line. And once written down it becomes a record that can follow a player into a contract conversation. The honest version of the same underlying need is a squad-planning question: exposure, availability history and minutes, taken to your medical lead. That is not medical advice either; it is the shape of the request that lets the people who give medical advice do their job.
Part 3 · the conversations

What this looks like in the room ◐ 8 min live + self-study

The rest of the session is the human half: the disagreement you will have, the policy you will write, the conversation nobody enjoys, and the commercial case for being the club that gets this right. Same caveat throughout, and it is not a formality: orientation only, not medical or legal advice.

LiveWhen a risk score disagrees with a clinician4 min▶

The answer to the headline question is short and not negotiable: the clinician decides. That is the whole of the answer, and the published recommendation is explicit that these outputs are supportive information integrated with clinical judgment, athlete history, wellness data, training context and staff expertise, never used in isolation for selection or return-to-play decisions.

The interesting question is a different one: what does the disagreement teach you?

  • Ask what the model can see that the clinician cannot. Sometimes it is a genuine exposure pattern across four weeks that no single consultation would surface. That is the model doing its actual job: raising something for a human to consider.
  • Ask what the clinician can see that the model cannot. Usually most of it. How the player moved this morning, what he said, what he did not say, what happened in his life this month.
  • If the model is never consulted, it has failed. If the model is never overruled, something is badly wrong. Both patterns are visible in a log and invisible in a memory.
LiveWriting the club's athlete-data policy4 min▶
  • What we collect, and why, purpose by purpose. One line each. If a purpose cannot be stated in one line, it has not been thought through.
  • Who sees what, using the four levels from Part 2, written as the club actually operates rather than as the org chart implies.
  • What we never do. The wall, stated plainly, including that medical detail does not enter contract talk without a documented, case-by-case basis.
  • How a player asks a question or objects, including a named person who is not their line manager and not the person who selects the team.

Your counsel owns the wording that binds you. This card is orientation for what to hand them, not a template to adopt.

LiveThe return-to-play conversation, and why a number is never the reason4 min▶
  • A number must never be the reason a player returns. Not because numbers are useless, but because the published performance range makes them unfit to carry a decision of that weight on their own, and because a person cannot appeal to a dashboard.
  • Watch for the number arriving as pressure rather than as information. "The model has him at low risk" said in a room with a director in it is not a neutral contribution, whatever the intent.
  • The player is in the conversation. They are the one who has to run at it, and what they tell you about how it feels is data your model does not have.
  • The consequences of getting this wrong land on one person's body, and it is not anyone in the meeting except the player.

Clinical decisions belong to clinicians. Nothing on this page is medical advice.

LiveGetting this right is a recruitment advantage3 min▶
  • Players talk to each other. Agents talk more. A club with a reputation for handling medical data carelessly acquires that reputation quickly and loses it slowly.
  • It is a genuine differentiator when you cannot compete on wages. "Here is exactly what we collect, here is who sees it, here is what we never do with it" is a concrete thing to say in a recruitment meeting, and very few clubs can say it.
  • It reduces the cost of the incident you have not had yet. Most clubs write their policy after something goes wrong, at maximum expense and minimum credibility.

Note the honesty required: the commercial case is real, and it is not the reason. The reason is that it is somebody's body. The commercial case is what gets the meeting.

Self-studyWhat players actually want to know about their data4 min read▶

Directors tend to prepare for the legal question and get asked the human one. The questions players raise, in roughly the order they raise them:

  • "Who sees this?" Almost always first, and the honest answer is usually longer than the club expected. Find out yours before you are asked in front of a squad.
  • "Will it be used against me in a contract negotiation?" This is the wall, and it is what the wall exists for. A vague answer here is worse than no policy at all.
  • "What happens to it when I leave?" Most clubs have not decided. Deciding is cheap now and expensive later.
  • "If I say no, what happens?" The hardest one, the most revealing one, and the one you should have a truthful answer to before a player asks it.
Homework

Try it yourself - this month ◐ 45-60 min total

  • Ask your medical lead one question: what would make you trust an analytics output? Do not argue, do not explain, do not offer a solution. Write down the answer in their words. That sentence is your specification.
  • Check who currently has access to raw wearable data at your club. Technical access, not permitted access. Compare the list to the four levels you wrote in Part 2 and note every gap.
  • Write the one sentence your club says to a player about their data. One sentence, plain language, no hedging. If you cannot write it, that is the finding, and it is the most useful thing you will learn this week.
  • Take the four-level map and the crossing-point definition to your club's counsel, and ask which parts do not hold in your jurisdiction, league or competition. Book that meeting before the next window.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · A vendor shows you an injury model reporting AUC 0.87 from a published study. What is the right reading of that number?

The spread in the literature is genuine inconsistency, not noise around a good answer, and results move with environment, team context and assessment methods. Ask for held-out performance on a squad the model has never seen, using your own definition of an injury. Whatever the number, the decision it would inform still belongs to your clinician. Orientation only, not medical advice.

2 · Your risk model flags a player as high risk. Your club doctor has assessed him and says he is fit to play. What happens?

The published recommendation is explicit that these outputs are supportive information integrated with clinical judgment, athlete history, wellness data, training context and staff expertise, and never used in isolation for selection or return-to-play decisions. Whose remit the decision sits in is settled in advance, not in the moment. The disagreement is still worth logging, because a model that is never overruled and one that is never consulted are both broken.

3 · A player's medical detail is about to be raised in a contract negotiation. What is the correct position?

The wall is the point of the access map, and the single crossing point is deliberately narrow: specific, documented, case by case, with the player informed and represented. What you can do today is find out whether your current tooling enforces the wall or merely expects it.
