Communicating to coaches

This is the session that decides whether every other session mattered. Go back to the analysis loop from b1: Capture, Model, Judge, Communicate, Decide. Analysis gets ignored at stage 4 far more often than it fails at stage 2, and the failure does not look like failure. It looks like a beautiful fourteen-page report, correctly modelled, properly held out, delivered on time, that nobody in the building opened. Rivermouth's head coach has never opened a dashboard. He will read one slide on the bus. Tonight you learn what goes on it, what gets cut, how to carry uncertainty in a sentence a coach can act on, and the phrases that lose a room in under five seconds.

Part 0

Why this is the session that counts

Seven sessions of method, and none of it reaches a pitch unless somebody acts on it. The uncomfortable arithmetic of an analytics department is this: a mediocre finding that gets used beats an excellent finding that gets filed. That is not an argument for lowering your standards at stage 2. It is an argument for spending as much care on stage 4 as you spent on the model, which almost nobody does, because stage 4 has no maths in it and feels like it should be easy. It is the hardest stage in the loop and it is where careers in this job are made.

Part 1 · the rule that governs everything else

The one-slide rule 7 min live

If it does not fit on one slide, the coach will not use it. Not because he is not clever and not because he is not interested, but because he has a training session in forty minutes, eleven other inputs, and a squad to pick. So the rule is: one idea, one number, one thing to do differently, and the evidence available underneath if he asks. The appendix is not a compromise. It is a separate document that exists to answer exactly one question, and the question is "how do you know?"

LiveWhat to cut, and why cutting it hurts4 min▶

Everything on the cut list is there because it is evidence you are competent, and that is exactly the reason it does not belong on the slide. The coach is not assessing your competence. He is deciding what to do at Thursday's session.

  • The method goes. He does not need to know it was a gradient-boosted anything. If the method matters to whether he should believe you, put it in the appendix and say "the working is behind this if you want it".
  • The caveat list goes. Not the uncertainty, which stays and gets rephrased in Part 2, but the list. Six caveats reads as "I am covering myself", and a coach hears that instantly.
  • Every chart you were proud of goes. This is the painful one. A chart that took a day and shows five things is a chart that shows nothing to a person reading for twenty seconds. If a chart survives, it shows one thing and its title is the finding.
  • The context pages go. Nobody has ever read page four of a fourteen-page report and changed a decision because of it.

What must survive, without exception: the count, the denominator, and the recommendation. A count without a denominator is a claim. A denominator without a recommendation is trivia. A recommendation without either is an opinion, and the coach already has plenty of those.

The twenty-second test Read the slide out loud to somebody who was not involved. If it takes longer than twenty seconds, or if they ask what the point is, it is not a slide yet.
LiveThe four things that must be on the slide3 min▶

Four elements, in this order, and nothing else above the line.

ElementWhat it looks likeWhat breaks without it
The finding, one sentence"They stop pressing goal kicks after the hour."He has to construct the point himself, and he will not
The count"11 of 14"It becomes a feeling, and his feeling beats yours in his own building
The denominator and the dates"of 14 goal kicks, since 2 March"He cannot judge whether it is a pattern or three matches
One thing to do differently"Hold the centre-backs back for their first two goal kicks after 60."Interesting is not actionable, and interesting is where analysis goes to die

One thing, not three. If you have three recommendations you have not finished the analysis, you have finished the arithmetic. Pick the one you would defend in front of the squad, and put the other two in the appendix where they can wait for a week when they are the main point.

Self-studyThe appendix that exists for one question2 min read▶

An appendix is not the place you put the report you wanted to write. It has exactly one job: when the coach or the assistant asks "how do you know?", you can answer completely in under a minute.

  • Method, in plain sentences. What you counted, over what, and how you decided what counted.
  • Sample and dates. How many matches, which ones, and what you excluded and why.
  • What would change your mind. The single most credibility-building line in any analytics document, and the one almost nobody writes.
  • One chart, at most. The one that makes the count visible. Not the one that took a day.

Keep it in the same file, one page behind the slide, so there is never a moment where you say "I can send that over". By the time you send it, the decision has been made.

Part 2 · saying what you do not know without losing the room

Uncertainty a coach can act on 8 min live

Saying "we are not sure" ends the conversation. Saying "in 11 of 14 goal kicks since they changed shape" starts one. Both sentences carry the same honesty about what you know; only one of them is useful. The skill is expressing uncertainty in units the coach already thinks in - matches, occasions, dates, players - rather than in units that belong to your discipline. Every published caveat in this track, from the injury-model range to the sample-size limits on shot data, can be said this way.

LiveFour phrasing patterns that carry uncertainty without abdicating5 min▶

Uncertainty is not a disclaimer you attach at the end. It is a property of the sentence itself. Four patterns, and between them they cover almost everything you will ever have to say.

  • 1 · Count with a denominator. "11 of 14 goal kicks." The uncertainty is right there in the three that went the other way, and the coach does the arithmetic himself in half a second. This is the single highest-value habit in the session.
  • 2 · A date range attached to the count. "Since 2 March, which is when they changed shape." Now he can judge whether it is a pattern or a fortnight, and he can tell you if something happened on 2 March that you did not know about. Half the time he can.
  • 3 · A range instead of a point estimate. "Somewhere between two and four extra chances a game" beats "2.8 extra chances a game", because the second one is a false precision he will remember and quote back at you when it is wrong.
  • 4 · What would change your mind. "If they keep their shape for the next three away games, I am wrong about this." This is the sentence that converts you from someone with an opinion into someone running a test, and coaches recognise the difference immediately.

What all four have in common: the uncertainty is expressed in things that happen on a pitch, not in things that happen in a model. Confidence intervals, p-values and AUC are all honest and all useless here, because they move the conversation onto ground where the coach cannot participate, and a coach who cannot participate stops listening.

LiveThe credibility mechanic: small and right, then big and uncertain4 min▶

There is an order to earning belief and it is not the order most analysts use. Most people lead with the biggest finding they have, because it is the most impressive. It is also the least verifiable, so it lands as a claim from someone the coach has no reason to trust yet.

The mechanic that actually works: be visibly right about something small, then visibly uncertain about something big, in that order.

  • Small and right, first. Something checkable within a week. "Their left-back does not follow the winger inside." He watches, it is true, and you have made a deposit.
  • Then big and uncertain. "I think there is something in how they defend the second phase, but I have got 14 occasions and I would want another five before I would build a session around it." He now believes the uncertainty because he has seen you be right when you were certain.
  • Why the reverse order fails. Lead with the big uncertain thing and the uncertainty reads as a hedge from someone who does not know. Same words, opposite effect, entirely because of what came before them.
  • Never be visibly certain about something you cannot check. One overclaim that gets disproved on a Saturday costs more than ten good weeks earn, and it is the mechanism by which a department loses a medical team or a head coach permanently.
Real world

This is why the injury numbers have to be said this way. Published injury-prediction models range from accuracy 52% and AUC 0.52 up to AUC 0.87, inconsistent across studies, with many landing at 0.63 to 0.65, which the literature itself calls poor. Said in that form to a head coach, it is noise. Said as "these models are right somewhere between a coin toss and quite good, depending on the study, which is why the recommendation everywhere is that they are supportive information and never a selection or return-to-play decision", it is a sentence a coach and a medical lead can both use. Same facts, and only the second version survives the meeting. This is not medical advice; it is how to be useful next to people who give it.

Build-along

Cut a real report to one slide ★ 20 min · everyone runs it

Open the last piece of work you sent to a coach. Not your best one, your last one. Work through the eight steps on the real thing, and keep the deleted material in a second file so the cutting feels survivable.

Find the one sentence. If your report contains three findings, pick the one you would defend in front of the squad. The other two are for another week.

Find the count. An actual integer of actual occasions. If you cannot produce one, you have a narrative rather than a finding, and that is the most useful thing you will learn tonight.

Find the denominator. Out of how many. Write it next to the count in the same phrase, never in a footnote.

Find the date range and say why it starts there. "Since 2 March, when they changed shape" is worth more than the count on its own.

Write one thing to do differently, phrased as an instruction a coach could give on Thursday. If it starts with "consider" or "monitor", it is not one yet.

Delete the method. All of it. Model name, features, validation, the two-number check. It goes to the appendix, where it belongs and where it is still yours.

Delete every chart but one, and only keep that one if its title is the finding. Move the rest to the appendix and do not look at them again.

Read it aloud in twenty seconds to someone who was not involved. If they ask what the point is, go back to step one. Then send only the slide.

The two prompts below are the same request. The first one produces a slide. The second one produces a smaller report, which is not the same thing and is arguably worse, because it looks finished.

The count and the denominator, in the same phrase (for example: 11 of 14) 3. The date range the count covers, and why it starts there 4. Exactly ONE thing to do differently, phrased as an instruction a coach could give at Thursday's session HARD RULES - Do NOT add any finding, number, cause or implication that is not already in the source. Bad - this gives you a shorter report Write an executive summary of this analysis.
Why the bad one fails An executive summary is a proportional shrink of everything you wrote, so it keeps the method, keeps the caveats, keeps a chart, and keeps the balance of a document nobody wanted. It also has no recommendation, because the source did not have one either. You end up with a well-written page that still asks the coach to work out what to do, which is the exact failure the one-slide rule exists to prevent.
Part 3 · the room, the words, and the format

Six things nobody tells a new analyst read alongside

The slide is the artefact. These six are the practice around it, and they are what separates an analyst people seek out from one people tolerate.

LiveThe language that loses the room4 min▶

Some words end a meeting in under five seconds, and none of them are wrong. They are simply in the wrong language for the room, and using them tells a coach that this conversation is happening on your terms rather than his.

Do not saySay instead
"The model has an AUC of 0.71""It gets this right more often than not, and I would not bet a selection on it"
"Feature importance shows pressure is the biggest driver""Being closed down matters more than distance on these chances"
"This is statistically significant""11 of 14, since March"
"We used a gradient-boosted classifier""I counted, and the working is behind this if you want it"
"The data suggests""I think, and here is the count"
"Directionally, we are seeing"Nothing. Wait until you have a count.

The word "significant" deserves its own warning. In its statistical sense it means one thing; in a coach's ear it means "important". You will be heard as saying the finding is important, which is a claim you did not make and might not be able to support. Never use it in a room. Say the count.

LiveWhen to say nothing at all3 min▶

The most underrated skill in this job is not producing analysis. It is knowing which weeks to produce none, and it is the fastest way to be taken seriously.

  • When the sample is too small and you know it. Three matches is not a pattern. Saying "I have not got enough yet, I will come back at ten games" is a deposit, not a failure.
  • When it is already obvious. If the coaching staff have seen it on video, confirming it with a number adds nothing and spends attention you will want later. Confirming the obvious repeatedly is how a department becomes wallpaper.
  • In the forty-eight hours after a bad defeat. Nothing you say will be heard as analysis. Send it Tuesday.
  • When you cannot name one thing to do differently. Interesting is not a reason to take up eight minutes of a staff meeting.
  • When somebody else in the building is about to say the same thing better. Let them. Then be the person who adds the count.

An analyst who sends something every week gets skimmed every week. An analyst who sends four things a season, each of which changed something, gets read on arrival.

LiveThe match report format - your b10 deliverable4 min▶

This is the format the whole track has been building toward, and in b10 you will build one for a real match and score it against every rail in the course. It is what a coach actually reads on the bus, which is to say: one screen, thumb-scrollable, no attachments.

  • Line 1 - the headline finding. One sentence, in his language, about this match.
  • Line 2 - the count, the denominator and the dates. Never a percentage on its own. "9 of 12 entries down our right" beats "75% of entries".
  • Line 3 - one thing to do differently, phrased as a session instruction.
  • Line 4 - what would change this. The honest limit, in matches or occasions, not in statistics.
  • Then, below a line: three clips, maximum. Timestamped, named, and each one showing the thing in line 2. Not a compilation.
  • Then the appendix, one page, answering "how do you know?" and nothing else.

Two rails carried forward from b1. Nothing in this report crosses the decision line: no selection call, no return-to-play view, no valuation. And every number on it has been through the two-number discipline before it got near the slide, even though neither number appears on the slide itself. The discipline is yours; the count is his.

Self-studyDelivering an answer the coach does not want3 min read▶

Sooner or later the number says his favourite player is the problem, or that the shape he designed is not working. How you handle that week determines whether you are ever asked a real question again.

  • Never in front of the room. A finding that embarrasses a coach in public will be rejected on the spot regardless of its quality, and you will not get a second hearing. One to one, first, always.
  • Lead with the question it answers, not the verdict. "You asked why we are conceding from our own throw-ins" is a very different opening from "our left-back is the problem".
  • Bring the count and the alternative explanations you already ruled out. He will reach for those explanations, and finding you already checked them is what makes the finding land.
  • Name what would make you wrong, honestly and first. It gives him somewhere to go that is not "your data is rubbish".
  • Then let it go. You reported it. The decision is his, it is on the decision line, and pushing a second time converts a finding into a fight.

The point is not to win. It is to make sure the decision gets made with the number in the room, and then to be the person who is still there next month.

Self-studyThe standing meeting versus the PDF2 min read▶

A document you send is a document that competes with everything else in an inbox. Ten minutes in a room that already exists competes with nothing.

  • Get onto an existing meeting. Do not create a new one for analytics. A meeting people already attend costs them nothing; a new one costs them a slot and their goodwill.
  • Ten minutes, one finding, same slot every week. Predictability is what turns you into part of the process rather than an interruption.
  • Send the slide after, not before. Sent before, it gets skimmed and your ten minutes becomes a recap. Sent after, it is a reminder of something they already discussed.
  • Watch what gets repeated. When a coach repeats your phrase back to the squad, that phrasing worked. Write it down and reuse it. Your best communication research happens in other people's mouths.
  • The PDF still has a job - the appendix, the record, the thing you point at in April. It is just not the delivery mechanism.
Self-studySame finding, three audiences3 min read▶

One finding, three rooms, three completely different sentences. The facts do not change; the unit the listener thinks in does.

AudienceWhat they needThe sentence
The playerWhat to do, in his own actions, with no judgement attached"When you receive facing your own goal, the pass inside is on more often than you are taking it. Have a look at these three."
The coachCount, denominator, dates, one session instruction"11 of 14 goal kicks since 2 March. Hold the centre-backs for their first two after the hour."
The directorWhat it changes, what it is worth, what the risk is"Three points a season of pattern, and it holds unless they change shape again. I will know within four matches."

The rule underneath all three: talk in the units of the decision the person actually makes. A player decides where to pass. A coach decides what to train. A director decides where money goes. Give any of them the other two versions and you will be politely thanked and quietly ignored.

Homework

Try it yourself - this week ◐ 30-40 min total

  • Take your last report and cut it to one slide, then send only that. Not the slide plus the report. Only the slide. Note what you feel when you press send, because that feeling is the thing you have to get over.
  • Rewrite three of your recent findings as counts with denominators and date ranges. If any of the three cannot be written that way, work out whether it was ever a finding.
  • Ask a coach what they actually did with your last piece of work, and write the answer down verbatim. Do not defend it, do not explain what you meant. The answer is the most valuable data you will collect this month.
  • Go through one report and delete every instance of "significant", "suggests", "directionally" and "may indicate". Replace each with a count or with nothing.
  • Write the four lines of your match report format on a card and use it for the next fixture. b10 will ask for it.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · Your fourteen-page opposition report was correct, properly held out and delivered on time, and nobody opened it. Where in the analysis loop did it fail?

A beautiful report nobody opened is the signature failure of stage 4. Blaming the model sends you back to improve something that was already fine; blaming the coach guarantees it happens again. The fix is the one-slide rule: one idea, one number, one thing to do differently, with the method and the caveats moved to an appendix that exists to answer one question.

2 · You have a real finding but a small sample and you are genuinely unsure. What do you put on the slide?

Hedged language carries no information and reads as covering yourself, so option A ends the conversation. Hiding the uncertainty entirely is the overclaim that costs a department its credibility on the first Saturday it goes wrong. A count with a denominator and a date range puts the uncertainty in units a coach thinks in, and "what would change my mind" turns you from someone with an opinion into someone running a test.

3 · You have one small checkable finding and one big uncertain one. What order do you present them in, and why?

The same words land completely differently depending on what came before them. Lead with the big uncertain claim and the uncertainty sounds like someone who does not know; lead with a small verifiable one and the same uncertainty sounds like discipline. Withholding uncertain findings entirely is its own failure, since the coach loses information he could have weighed. What you must never do is be visibly certain about something he cannot check.
