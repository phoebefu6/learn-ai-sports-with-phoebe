Game film and auto-tagging

You have just spent a session building a shot model. Now meet the uncomfortable truth about your job: the head coach is never going to look at it. He is going to watch clips. Auto-tagging turns ninety minutes of unsearchable footage into an index you can query in seconds, and the moment it does, your job stops being finding clips and becomes choosing them - which is stage 3 of the loop, the judgment stage, the one no model does for you and the one that decides whether he opens your file next week. Tonight: the six-clip discipline, a twenty-event audit that tells you your own tool's error rate, and the honest ranking of which events a tagger can actually find. Rivermouth's video archive is the one nobody can search. That changes this week.

Part 0

Where tonight sits in the loop

Auto-tagging is Capture, and machines are strong there. Searching the index is Model, and machines are strong there too. Then you hit the wall. Deciding which six of the four hundred candidate moments a coach should see on Friday is Judge, and a model has no idea what your squad is working on, what he said in the last meeting, or which player will stop listening if you show that clip. Cutting and framing them is Communicate, where a model helps. Whether anything changes on Saturday is Decide, and that is his. Tonight is mostly about stage 3, dressed up as a video session. The examples are football because the public evidence base is deepest there. The pattern transfers to any sport with footage.

Part 1 · the actual product

The clip pack is the product 7 min live

Coaches do not read models. They watch clips, and then they decide whether the person who sent the clips is worth listening to. Auto-tagging changes the economics completely: ninety minutes becomes a searchable index, and the hours you used to spend scrubbing a timeline come back to you. What you do with those hours is the whole point. The scarce resource was never finding footage - it was choosing, and choosing is a judgment job that the tagging model cannot touch.

LiveSix clips, one idea, and the Saturday question5 min▶

The discipline is short enough to write on a whiteboard and hard enough that most departments never hold it.

  • Six clips maximum. Not because six is magic, but because a cap forces a ranking, and a ranking forces you to decide what you actually believe. A pack of twenty is you refusing to choose and handing the work to the coach.
  • One idea. Every clip in the pack argues the same point. Two ideas is two packs, delivered in two weeks. A pack that covers pressing, set-piece marking and build-up is a highlights reel with a job title.
  • Every clip survives the Saturday question. "What do you want them to do differently on Saturday?" If the honest answer is "notice this" or "see how bad that was", it is not a coaching clip, it is a complaint.
  • Cut the clip that only proves you were right. There is always one. It is in the pack for you, not for them, and it is the one that costs you the room.
  • The metric is not clips produced. It is whether the coach opened last week's file, and whether he asked for this week's.
Self-studyWhy choosing is judgment, not filtering3 min read▶

A filter is a rule you can write down. Judgment is what is left when the rules run out, and clip selection is full of it.

  • Context the index does not hold. What the staff worked on in training this week, what was said in the last meeting, which instruction was actually given. None of that is in the footage or the tags.
  • Squad reality. The clearest example of the problem might involve a player who is out, or one whose confidence is fragile, or one the coach has already spoken to twice. That is not a data question.
  • Selecting on outcome is the classic error. The moments that led to goals are not the moments that best demonstrate the behaviour. If your pool is sorted by what happened next, you are teaching results, not football.
Part 2 · check it like you would any model

Tagging is a model, so check it like one 7 min live

Auto-tagging accuracy varies enormously by event type, and the variation is not random - it tracks how clearly the event is defined and how visible it is. A shot is easy: the ball leaves a foot toward a goal, and there is a well-agreed definition of what that is. A pressing trigger is hard, because it is a coaching concept before it is a visual event. And an off-ball run is a definition argument before it is a detection problem at all - two analysts at the same club will disagree about whether one happened.

LiveThe twenty-event review pass5 min▶

This is mandatory and it is cheap, which is a rare combination. It takes under an hour and it is the difference between using a tool and trusting a brochure.

  • Sample twenty tagged events of one type at random from a match. Random, not the first twenty, and not the ones you remember.
  • Watch each one and mark it right or wrong. Two failure modes to record separately: the tag is on something that is not that event, and the tag is on the right moment but the label is wrong.
  • Then look for what it missed. Watch a fifteen-minute block properly and count the events of that type the tool did not tag at all. Misses are invisible in a sample of what it found, and they are usually the bigger problem.
  • Do it per event type. One overall accuracy number for a tagging tool is meaningless in exactly the way b1 warned about, because the tool is five different tools wearing one name.
Real world

This is the two-number discipline applied to somebody else's model. A tagging tool's advertised accuracy is the fitted number: measured by the vendor, on footage they chose, with definitions they wrote. Your twenty-event audit is the held-out number, on your camera angles, with your definitions. If the two are close, the tool travels well. If the gap is large, you have not caught the vendor lying - you have found out that your definitions differ from theirs, which is the b2 problem arriving with a subscription attached.

LiveWhat to do with an error rate once you have one3 min▶

The number is not the deliverable. What changes because of the number is the deliverable.

  • High-reliability types become searchable infrastructure. Shots and set pieces you can query and trust, which is what makes the archive worth building.
  • Low-reliability types never go straight to a coach. A pressing trigger tag is a suggestion for you to check, and nothing more.
  • Never build a count on an unmeasured type. "We pressed 42 times" from a tagger you have not audited is a made-up number with a decimal point's worth of confidence.
Part 3 · the craft around the clips

Five things that decide whether it lands reference

Context length, the archive, the tone you use with a player, the opposition pack, and the honest verdict on live in-match analysis.

LiveClip context, and the two seconds that change everything4 min▶

Where a clip starts is an argument, not a setting. Cut two seconds later and you have shown a different piece of football.

  • Lead-in shows the cause. A defender caught out of position looks like a mistake if you start when the pass is played, and looks like a consequence of the previous action if you start five seconds earlier. Both clips are honest footage. Only one is honest analysis.
  • Set a default and hold it. Five seconds before, three after, adjusted only with a reason you could say out loud. Inconsistent lead-ins across a pack quietly make some players look worse than others.
  • This is the media clip problem, indoors. Everyone can spot a broadcaster clipping a quote to change its meaning. The same edit, applied to a clip of your left back, is harder to spot and lands on someone who works with you.
LiveTurning an unsearchable archive into a searchable one4 min▶

Rivermouth has years of footage nobody can query, which means it is storage, not an asset. Turning it around is a project with a very clear sequence.

  • Start with the reliable event types. Shots and set pieces first. They tag well, they are what people search for most, and an archive that answers those two questions already earns its keep.
  • Store the tag, the timecode and the match identity together, and use the field names from your b2 data dictionary. An index with its own private vocabulary is a second source of truth.
  • What it unlocks: opposition prep in minutes rather than days, every instance of a pattern instead of the three you remember, player development clips pulled across a whole season, and recruitment footage of a target's actual behaviour rather than his highlights.
LivePlayer-facing clips - the tone that lands4 min▶

A clip pack shown to a squad is a piece of communication about a person, in front of their colleagues. The football content can be perfect and the session can still do damage.

  • Show the behaviour, not the humiliation. The moment that best demonstrates the point is often not the worst moment. Pick the clearest one, not the most painful one.
  • Pair a correction with the same player doing it well. Two clips, same player, same session. It is not softening the message; it proves the behaviour is available to him.
  • Name the situation, not the character. "The far-side cover is late here" is coachable. "He switches off" is a verdict, and nobody improves after receiving one in a group setting.
  • You are not the coach, and the room is not yours. Bring the options and the honest read. The decision about what gets shown, and how, sits with the staff - which is stage 5, and b1 was clear about who owns that.
LiveOpposition packs are a different product3 min▶

Same tooling, different job, and the mistakes are different too. b6 is the full session; here is what carries over from tonight.

  • The six-clip discipline still holds, and it is harder, because there is more to say about a team you have not been working with all year.
  • Distinguish a tendency from a plan. Something a team does often is not the same as something they intend, and only one of those survives a change of opponent. b6 is built around this line.
  • Say what to do about it. An opposition pack that describes without prescribing is trivia. Every clip still owes an answer to the Saturday question.
Self-studyLive in-match analysis, and why most of it is not worth doing4 min read▶

Real-time analysis is the most requested and least defensible thing a video department does. The honest position, following straight on from b2's point about sample size:

  • Rates over twenty minutes are noise. Any percentage delivered to a bench at half time is a reading of variance, presented at the moment nobody has the bandwidth to challenge it.
  • The tagging error rate is worse live. No time for the review pass, so you are handing unverified model output over at the highest-pressure moment of the week.
  • What genuinely is worth doing: a clip somebody asked for, a factual state check such as bookings and minutes, and a pre-agreed trigger the staff named before kick-off.
  • Agree the list before the match, in writing. The reason live analysis goes wrong is that nobody defined it, so it becomes whatever the analyst noticed. Two agreed outputs beat ten improvised ones.
Build-along

Cut a six-clip pack from last match ★ 22 min · everyone runs it

Footage from your most recent match, your tagging tool, and one idea. By the end of this you will have a pack you could send tonight, and more usefully, a written reason for every clip that is in it and every clip that is not.

Write the idea first, in one sentence, before you open the footage. "We are conceding the second ball after our own corners." If you cannot write it in one sentence you are not ready to cut clips, you are still exploring.

Query the index for the candidate pool. Note how many candidates the tool returned and which event types you relied on. If any of them are from the unreliable end of diagram 2, you are hand-verifying every one.

Verify the pool. Watch the candidates and drop the mis-tags. Count how many you dropped - that is a live sample of your tool's error rate on this event type.

Rank by how directly each moment demonstrates the idea, not by outcome and not by timeline order. Say the ranking out loud to a colleague if there is one in the room.

Take the top six and set your lead-in. Five seconds before, three after, the same for every clip. Where you deviate, write down why.

Run the Saturday question on each one. What do you want them to do differently? Any clip without a clean answer comes out and is not replaced. A five-clip pack is fine. A padded six is not.

Good prompt · shortlist candidates, do not choose them 
Here is a tagged event index for our match on Saturday, with event type,
timecode, players involved and pitch location: [paste index].

The idea for this week's pack: we are conceding the second ball after our
own corners.

Shortlist up to 12 candidate moments that DEMONSTRATE this behaviour.
For each: the timecode, how many seconds of lead-in are needed for the
cause to be visible, and one line on what it shows.

Rules:
- Do NOT rank or select on outcome. A moment that led to nothing is as
  eligible as one that led to a shot against us.
- If a tag is one you cannot verify from the index alone, mark it
  "needs eyes" rather than including it silently.
- I am choosing the final six, not you. Give me candidates, not a pack.
Bad prompt · outsources the judgment 
Give me the best moments from this match.
Why the good one works It keeps the model at stages 1 and 2, where it is strong, and takes the selection back for stage 3. "Best" has no meaning without an idea and a Saturday question, so a model asked for the best moments will quietly substitute the only ranking it has: what happened next.
Homework

Try it yourself - this week ◐ 60-90 min total

  • Run a twenty-event tagging audit and write down your tool's error rate by event type. Record wrong-moment and wrong-label separately, then watch a fifteen-minute block to count what it missed entirely.
  • Cut one six-clip pack on one idea, with a consistent lead-in and a written reason for each clip. Note which clip you nearly included because it proved you were right.
  • Ask a coach which of the six they would actually use, and which they would not. Do not defend the pack. Write the answer down - it is the most useful feedback in this session.
  • Agree a live in-match list with your staff before the next fixture: at most two outputs, in writing, decided before kick-off.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · Your auto-tagger's vendor advertises 94% accuracy. What does that number entitle you to claim about your own footage?

This is the b1 two-number discipline pointed at somebody else's model. The advertised figure was measured on footage they chose, with definitions they wrote, and it is a single number for what is really five different detectors. Sample twenty events per type on your own camera angles, and count the misses separately.

2 · You have four hundred tagged candidates and a coach meeting on Friday. Where does the real work of this session sit?

Auto-tagging is strong at Capture and the query is Model, both of which the machine does well. Choosing requires knowing what was worked on in training, what was said last week and which player the message will actually reach. That is judgment, it is the stage machines are weak at, and it is the reason the coach opens your file next week.

3 · A clip shows your left back badly out of position. Starting it five seconds earlier reveals that the midfielder ahead of him vacated the space. Which version goes in the pack?

Where a clip starts is an argument, not a setting. The short cut is honest footage and dishonest analysis, and inconsistent lead-ins across a pack quietly make some players look worse than others. Set a default, hold it, and deviate only with a reason you could say out loud. A pattern that only survives one exact start point is a weak pattern.
