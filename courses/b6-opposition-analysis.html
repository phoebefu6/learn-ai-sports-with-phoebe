Opposition analysis

Opposition work is where an analytics department is most likely to be believed and most likely to be wrong, because the output looks like insight and the sample is tiny. Five matches is not a season. Half of what looks like a team's identity is one player, one opponent's approach, one scoreline they were chasing, or a shape they abandoned three weeks ago. Tonight you learn the discipline that survives contact with a coach: separate what a team does from what a team intends, and give every single claimed tendency a count, a denominator and a date range. As a sense of scale for how certain even good models get: post-match expected goals predicts match outcome at around 0.656 accuracy over a whole match. Your five-match pattern is not going to do better than that.

Part 1 · the core discipline

A tendency is not a plan 8 min live

There is a difference between what a team does and what a team intends, and the data only ever shows you the first one. A team that pressed the goalkeeper in eleven of its last fourteen goal kicks might have a pressing plan, or might have been chasing games, or might have played three opponents who invited it. Your job is not to guess which. Your job is to cut the claim until only the defensible part is left, and then say that part precisely.

LiveThe four traps6 min▶

Almost every false tendency in an opposition pack is one of these four. Learn to run them as a checklist and you will cut your pack in half in twenty minutes, which is the correct outcome.

  • A pattern that is really one player. "They switch play early" is often one left-sided midfielder who does it and four teammates who do not. If that player is suspended, injured or dropped on Saturday, you have briefed the squad on a tendency that will not appear. Always cut the pattern by who executed it.
  • A pattern that is really one opponent's approach. A team looks like it plays direct when three of its last five opponents pressed it high. That is not their identity, it is their response. Cut by opponent and see whether the pattern survives against teams that sat off.
  • A pattern from a match they were losing from minute 20. A side chasing a two-goal deficit for seventy minutes generates a huge volume of events that look like aggression, and it will dominate any aggregate over five matches. Cut by scoreline state, and cut by game state as it actually was minute by minute, not by the final result.
  • A pattern that disappeared when they changed shape three weeks ago. The most dangerous one, because the data is real and the team is gone. A new manager, a returning centre-half, a switch to a back three - any of these can make everything before the change actively misleading. Always find the change date first, then decide how much history you are allowed to use.
The rule that catches all four Every claimed tendency needs a count, a denominator and a date range. "11 of 14 goal kicks, in the four matches since 8 November." You cannot write that sentence without having done the cuts, which is exactly why the rule works. Anything you cannot express in that form does not go in the pack.
LiveDoes versus intends, and why the difference matters4 min▶

Coaches think in intentions, because that is what they can prepare against. Data records behaviour. Collapsing the two is the most common way an analyst says something confidently false.

What the data showsWhat it might meanWhat you may claim
Wide players stay high when the full back has the ballA designed structure, or two wingers who do not track backThe behaviour, with a count, and who did it
Long goal kicks in 11 of 14A plan, or a response to being pressed, or a keeper who is uncomfortableThe count and the denominator, plus what the opponent was doing
Fouls cluster in the first fifteen minutesAn instruction to set a tone, or noise on 5 matchesUsually nothing. This is the classic small-sample mirage
They score lateFitness, substitutions, opponents tiring, or coincidenceThe count, and an explicit note that you cannot separate the causes

The useful phrasing is behavioural and dated: "since the shape change they have done X in Y of Z situations". If a coach wants to interpret that as an intention, that is his job and he is better at it than you are. Handing him a behaviour he can verify on video is worth far more than handing him a theory.

Self-studyFinding the change date before you find anything else3 min read▶

Before you run a single query, establish what has changed about this opponent and when. It takes fifteen minutes and it determines which matches you are even allowed to pool.

  • Manager change, and the first match under the new one. Everything before it is a different team wearing the same shirt.
  • Formation or shape change, which you can usually spot from average positions or simply from the team sheets.
  • A key player returning or leaving. A first-choice holding midfielder coming back from six weeks out changes more of the data than most tactical shifts.
  • Fixture context. A midweek European trip, a derby, a match after a manager was publicly backed. Not everything, but worth knowing before you explain an anomaly with tactics.

Write the change date at the top of the pack. Every claim underneath it then carries a date range for free, and you have made the hardest discipline in the session automatic.

Part 2 · saying it so it holds up

Sample size and what to tell the coach 7 min live

There is no magic threshold where a pattern becomes true, and anyone who gives you one is selling something. What there is instead is a working practice: report the count and the denominator every time, and let the coach see how thin the evidence is. The single most misleading thing an analyst produces is a percentage on a small denominator, because a percentage hides its own sample size by design. "78%" and "11 of 14" carry identical information and produce completely different behaviour in the room.

LiveHow many observations before it is worth a slide4 min▶

There is no clean answer, so here is the working one that keeps analysts out of trouble:

  • Under about ten occurrences, do not put a rate on it at all. Show the count and, better, show the clips. Six of eight is a thing a coach can watch. "75%" on eight events is a number that will be quoted back to you in a press conference.
  • Set pieces are the exception that proves the rule, because they are the most repeated, most sampled and most rehearsed situation in the sport. You get more observations of a corner routine in five matches than of almost anything else, which is why they are the highest-value part of opposition work.
  • Prefer a stable denominator. "Per goal kick" is countable and comparable. "Per match" hides that one match had 14 goal kicks and another had 4.
  • Never combine across the change date to get your sample up. That is the temptation the whole discipline exists to resist. A bigger sample of the wrong team is worse than a small sample of the right one.
  • When the sample is too thin, say so and stop. "We do not have enough of these to say anything" is a finding, it takes four seconds, and it is the sentence that makes the other three tendencies believable.
Real world

Calibrate against what a good model actually achieves. Post-match expected goals - built on a full match of shots, with the result already known - predicts match outcome at around 0.656 accuracy. That is a genuinely useful model with a whole match of evidence behind it, and it is right roughly two times in three. Your five-match tendency, built from fourteen goal kicks, is nowhere near that. Say "informative" and never say "certain", and the coach will trust the next thing you bring.

LiveThe phrasing that survives the room3 min▶

Same finding, three registers. Read them out loud, in order, and notice what happens to your own confidence:

  • "They always press the goalkeeper." Falsified the first time they do not. Once that happens on Saturday, everything else you said becomes suspect.
  • "They press about 70% of goal kicks." Better, and still unusable, because he cannot tell whether that is 7 of 10 or 70 of 100, and he cannot tell whether it is from this version of the team.
  • "In the four matches since they changed shape, in 11 of 14 goal kicks." Precise, dated, checkable, and honest about the size. A coach can rehearse against that, and if it does not happen on Saturday you have lost nothing, because you never claimed it would.

Three words to strike from opposition packs entirely: always, never, and tends to. The first two are falsified by a single counterexample. The third sounds cautious and is actually the worst of them, because it makes an unfalsifiable claim while appearing modest. b8 takes this further into the one-slide rule and the language of uncertainty a coach can act on.

Part 3 · the pack and the room

Building something a coach uses cards · mixed

The best opposition pack in the league is worth nothing if it arrives as 22 slides on Thursday night. These five cards cover the shape of the deliverable, the highest-value area to spend your time on, and the two conversations that make opposition work politically difficult.

LiveBuilding the opposition pack4 min▶

The pack is not a report, it is a rehearsal aid. Structure it the way the week runs:

  • Three tendencies. Not eleven. Each with a count, a denominator and a date range, each with the clips attached. b4's six-clip rule applies directly: a coach will watch six clips properly and will skim sixteen.
  • One page the staff can hold, then the clips, then the appendix nobody opens but everybody is reassured exists. b8 makes this the one-slide rule and it is the single biggest determinant of whether your work gets used.
  • Lead with the change date. "This is the team since 8 November" frames every number that follows and pre-empts the most common objection.
  • Attach the counterexamples yourself. Include the three goal kicks where they did not press. A coach who finds them himself stops trusting you; a coach you hand them to starts.
  • Deliver it when it can change something. Thursday morning, not Friday night. Analysis delivered after the plan is written is a critique, not a contribution.
LiveSet pieces: the highest-value part of the pack4 min▶

If you have limited hours before Saturday, spend them here. Set pieces are the one area of opposition work where the sample size problem is genuinely milder and the coaching payoff is direct.

  • They are the most repeated situation in the sport. Corners, attacking free kicks, throw-ins in the final third and goal kicks recur many times per match, which gives you a real denominator inside five matches instead of a handful of events.
  • They are rehearsed, so they are stable. A routine a team has drilled is far less sensitive to opponent, scoreline and shape than open-play behaviour is. The four traps still apply, they just bite less hard.
  • They are directly actionable. A coach can put eleven players on a training pitch on Friday and rehearse against a specific corner delivery. Very little else you produce converts into training-ground time that cleanly.
  • They are countable in a way everyone accepts. "Nine of their last fourteen corners went to the near post" is a sentence with no interpretation in it, which means no argument about it.
  • Still date them. Set-piece routines change when the coach changes, and a new set-piece specialist can rewrite the whole catalogue in a fortnight.
LiveWhen your analysis says the opponent is better3 min▶

Occasionally the honest read is that Saturday's opponent is stronger than the staff believe. This is professionally awkward and there is a right way to handle it:

  • Never lead with the verdict. "They are better than us" is a judgment, and judgments are the head coach's territory. Lead with the specific, dated, counted behaviour that produced your view.
  • Convert it into a question about preparation, not a prediction about the result. "Their build-up beat a high press in 9 of 12 attempts since the shape change" is preparation. "We will struggle" is a forecast nobody asked you for.
  • Bring the thing they do worst in the same breath. Every team has one. Balance is not spin here, it is completeness, and it is what makes the uncomfortable part hearable.
  • Say it once, privately, before the meeting. The same rule as the medical department in b5: never surprise a decision maker in front of an audience.
  • Accept being overruled. The coach decides what the team does. Your job was to make sure the decision was made with the information, not to win.
LiveScouting individuals without writing a dossier3 min▶

Individual opposition profiles are where packs go to die. Eleven one-page profiles is eleven pages nobody reads. Compress ruthlessly:

  • Three or four players, maximum. The ones whose behaviour actually changes what your players should do. Everyone else gets a line in a table.
  • One actionable trait each, counted and dated. "Comes inside onto his left in 12 of 15 one-on-ones since November" is usable. "Technically gifted, good feet" is a scouting cliche with no instruction inside it.
  • Say what your player does about it. A trait without a response is trivia. Show it to the coach and let him supply the instruction, then write the instruction next to the trait.
  • Watch the trap-one problem in reverse. If your team's whole plan hinges on one opponent, have a version of the pack for when he does not start. Two lines is enough.
  • Recruitment-style evaluation is a different job. Judging whether a player is good, rather than what he does on Saturday, is b7. Do not let an opposition pack drift into a valuation.
Self-studyHow the opponent is scouting you4 min read▶

Every claim in this session applies to the analyst sitting in the away dressing room on Saturday, running the same queries on your matches. Taking that seriously changes three things about how you work:

  • Your own tendencies are visible and countable. Run your pack on yourself once a month. If your side takes a short corner in 10 of 13, somebody has noticed, and it is better that you notice first.
  • Predictability has a cost and a benefit. A rehearsed routine is effective partly because it is rehearsed. The question is never "are we predictable" but "is this pattern still winning us the situation now that they know about it". That is answerable with a count.
  • Watch what opponents change against you. If three consecutive opponents suddenly stopped pressing your goal kicks, they are all reading the same thing about you. Their behaviour is a free read on your own scouting report.
  • A variation is worth more than a secret. Secrets last one match. Having two versions of the same routine survives being scouted, and it is a much easier sell to a coach than "stop doing the thing that works".
  • Apply your own standard. If you would reject a claim about the opponent for having no denominator, reject it about yourself too. Self-scouting is where analysts are least disciplined, because nobody is checking.
Build-along

Three verified tendencies ★ 22 min · everyone builds it

Open Saturday's opponent and their last five matches. By the end of this you will have three claims you are prepared to defend, and a list of the ones you threw away. The discarded list is not waste, it is the evidence that the three survivors are real.

Find the change date before anything else. Manager, shape, a returning first-choice player. Write it at the top of the page. Everything from here is dated relative to it, and matches before it are a different team.

Write down every pattern you think you have seen, from memory and from the video, without checking any of them. Eight to twelve is normal. This list exists to be cut.

Give each one a denominator you can count. Not "per match" - per goal kick, per corner, per one-on-one, per build-up from the back. Anything you cannot attach a countable denominator to is deleted now.

Run the four cuts on the survivors: by player, by opponent, by scoreline state minute by minute, and by the change date. Watch the counts collapse. Most claims will not survive two cuts.

Keep the best three and write each as one sentence in the form "in the N matches since date, in X of Y situations". If you cannot write the sentence, the tendency is not ready and it does not go in the pack.

Attach up to six clips per tendency, including the counterexamples, and hand it over on Thursday morning. Then ask the coach which of the three changed anything about Friday's session.

Why three A coach has a finite number of instructions he can give the squad before Saturday, and it is smaller than you think. Three dated, counted tendencies with clips get rehearsed. Eleven get skimmed, and the two good ones drown with the nine weak ones. Cutting is not modesty here, it is the deliverable.
Good prompt - forces the count, the denominator and the date 
You are helping me verify opposition tendencies from event data. The
opponent changed shape on 8 November; four of the five matches attached
are after that date and one is before.

For every pattern you report, you MUST give all three of:
  count · denominator · date range
in the form "in the N matches since <date>, in X of Y <situations>".
If you cannot produce all three from the data, do not report the pattern
at all - list it under "not verifiable" instead.

For each reported pattern also give:
  - how many different players executed it, and their names
  - how it splits by scoreline state (level, ahead, behind)
  - whether it appears in the pre-8-November match

Forbidden: the words always, never, tends to, usually, often, likes to,
prefers, typically. Do not report any pattern with a denominator under 8
& do not aggregate across 8 November to increase a sample size.

Output at most 6 candidate patterns, ranked by denominator, largest first.

The banned-word list is doing more work than it looks. Removing "always", "never" and "tends to" removes the only grammar in which an unverified claim can be stated, so the model is left with counts. The "not verifiable" bucket matters just as much: without somewhere to put the failures, a language model will quietly upgrade a weak pattern rather than return less than you asked for.

Bad prompt - do not send this 
Here are the last five matches. What are the key tendencies of this team?
Give me the top five things they like to do so I can build the pack.

This produces a confident, fluent, well-written page of things that may or may not have happened. "Key" invites the model to decide what matters, "like to do" asks for intention rather than behaviour, "top five" guarantees five answers whether or not five exist, and nothing anywhere requires a denominator or a date. Every one of the four traps passes straight through it, and the output reads well enough that nobody in the meeting will question it - which is the actual danger.

Homework

Try it yourself - this week ◐ 30-40 min total

  • Take one claim from your last opposition pack and find its count, denominator and date range. Pick the one you were most confident about. If you cannot produce all three, you have learned the most useful thing available to you this week.
  • Cut one pack down to three tendencies. Keep the discarded list and the reason each one was cut - by player, by opponent, by scoreline, by date.
  • Ask the coach which of the three changed anything about how he prepared the team. Do not defend the other two. Write the answer down and use it to choose what you look at next time.
  • Run your own pack on your own team once, and find the tendency you would flag if you worked for Saturday's opponent.
  • Strike always, never and tends to from every document you own. Rewrite each affected sentence with a count and a date range, and notice how many will not survive the rewrite.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · Across the opponent's last five matches, they pressed the goalkeeper on 31 of 44 goal kicks. What do you check first?

The four cuts come before anything else, because they decide which matches you are even allowed to pool. Here the shape change three weeks ago removes one match and the chase periods remove more, and 31 of 44 becomes 11 of 14 since the change. Sample size questions asked before the cuts are asked about the wrong sample.

2 · You have a verified finding. Which version goes to the head coach?

A percentage hides its own denominator, and "tends to" makes a claim that cannot be checked while sounding cautious. "Always" is falsified the first time it does not happen, and takes the rest of your pack down with it. The count with a denominator and a date range is precise, checkable, honest about how thin it is, and something a coach can rehearse against on Friday.

3 · Your model of the opponent's build-up is strong and you want to tell the coach how confident to be. What is the right framing?

Around 0.656 accuracy is what a good model achieves with a complete match of evidence and the result already known. Anything built on fourteen goal kicks across four matches is much less certain than that, and saying so is what makes the next pack believable. A coach who is told how sure to be can plan; a coach given a bare conclusion has to guess how much of it to trust.
