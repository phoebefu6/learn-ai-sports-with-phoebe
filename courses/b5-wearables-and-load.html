Part 0

Where you stand tonight

Read this first: this session is orientation for working alongside clinicians, and it is not medical advice. Nothing here is a protocol, a screening tool or a rehabilitation input. At Rivermouth FC the medical department has been burned by a dashboard before. Someone built a traffic-light screen, a player was quietly left out because of an amber square, and the physio found out in front of the coach. That is why the door is shut. Part 1 · the evidence, unflattered

The honest picture, in numbers 8 min live

You will be asked for an injury model. Possibly this month. The way you answer decides whether the medical department ever works with you again, so it is worth knowing the literature better than the person asking. Published machine-learning injury-prediction performance runs from accuracy 52% and AUC 0.52 at the bottom to AUC 0.87 at the top, it is inconsistent across studies, and a large cluster sits at AUC 0.63 to 0.65, which the reviews themselves describe as poor. Both ends of that range are real. Quoting only the top end is the single most common way an analyst loses a medical department in one meeting.

LiveFour reasons the numbers look like that5 min▶

None of these are reasons to give up. They are reasons to know what you are holding.

  • Injuries are rare, so the data is imbalanced. Low injury incidence limits model performance directly. With a rare outcome, a model drifts toward the safe and useless prediction that nobody gets hurt, and on plain accuracy that looks excellent. This is why accuracy alone is close to meaningless here.
  • Generalisability is weak. Results move with environment, team context and how things were assessed. A model published on another squad, in another league, with another definition of what counts as an injury, is a hypothesis at your club, not a tool you install.
  • GPS-derived workload is context dependent. It captures short-term exposure genuinely well, and short-term exposure is not the whole story of why a hamstring goes. Treating a load number as a cause is the mistake the wearable vendors are least motivated to correct.
  • Relatively few studies exploit full tracking granularity. The richest data in the building is under-used in the published work, which is one of the more interesting open gaps and also a reason to be humble about what the literature has actually tested.

Say it in one sentence when asked: the published range runs from about a coin toss to genuinely strong, it does not transfer well between clubs, and the reviews are explicit that the output is supportive information only. That sentence has never once made a physio think less of an analyst.

LivePrior injury: the baseline that has to be beaten4 min▶

The strongest single predictor of a future muscle injury is unglamorous and free: prior injury, with hazard and odds ratios often above 2.0. It needs no wearable, no vendor and no model. It is sitting in the medical department's own records.

That gives you a test with real teeth, and it is the same two-number discipline from b1 applied to a much more serious question:

  • Build the boring baseline first. Rank the squad by prior injury history alone. Write down how it performs on a season the model has never seen.
  • Then test the sophisticated model against it, on the same held-out season, on the same definition of injury.
  • If it does not clear the baseline, it has not earned its place. Not in the building, not in a meeting, and above all not in a room where somebody is deciding whether a player trains tomorrow.

Most vendor demonstrations you will be shown never make this comparison. Asking for it politely, in writing, is one of the most useful things an analyst does all year.

Real world

Why the baseline test matters more here than anywhere else in the track. On a shot model, an overfitted number costs you credibility. Here the same failure mode - a model that learned this squad rather than the game - attaches to a person's body and career. The decision line from b1 exists for exactly this: selection, return to play and valuation are three calls a model never makes alone, and return to play is the one where the gap between model quality and stakes is widest.

Part 2 · your actual job

What an analyst can honestly deliver 7 min live

Here is the move that changes everything about this area of work: stop trying to predict an event and start describing an exposure. You are not building a forecast of who gets hurt. You are building an availability and exposure picture - minutes load, fixture congestion, prior-injury history, time since return to full training - and those describe things that already happened, which you can be accurate about. They then answer real planning questions, which is what was being asked for in the first place.

LiveThe four columns, and the questions they answer6 min▶

Everything in the availability picture describes the past. That is the source of its honesty, and it is also why nobody can argue with it.

ColumnWhat it isWhat it does not claim
Minutes loadMinutes played over the last 28 and 90 daysNot a threshold, not a ceiling, not a cause
CongestionMatches per rolling 8 and 24 day window, plus travelNot a safe or unsafe verdict on a fixture
Prior injuryWhether this player has had this kind of injury beforeNot a diagnosis, not a prognosis, not a probability
Time since returnDays since return to full training, as recorded by medicalNot an opinion on whether the return was right

Four columns of counted history. No score, no ranking, no colour. The moment you add a fifth column that combines them into one number, you have built the thing the evidence says you should not build, and the medical department will read it as exactly that, correctly. What those four columns buy you is the set of questions a coach and a medical lead were actually trying to settle when they asked for an injury model, none of which need a prediction:

  • "How do we get through a congested December?" Eight matches in 24 days is a planning problem with a countable structure: who has ever done this before, where the two-day turnarounds fall, which positions have no cover at all.
  • "Who has never played three matches in eight days?" That is a count from history, not a forecast. It tells the staff where they are stepping into the unknown, which is genuinely useful and completely honest.
  • "What does this schedule do to this squad?" Rivermouth has a 19-man effective squad and two players who have played every minute. The fixture list is a fact and the squad is a fact, and putting them side by side is analysis nobody else in the building has time to do.
The standing recommendation, stated plainly Model and analytical outputs are supportive information, to be integrated with clinical judgment, athlete history, wellness data, training context and staff expertise, and are never used in isolation for selection or return-to-play decisions. Put that sentence on the picture itself, in the same font size as everything else, not as small print.
Part 3 · the department that does not trust you

Working next to clinicians cards · mixed

Technical skill is not what is blocking you here. A medical department that has been burned by a dashboard is not making a technical objection, it is making an accurate one: somebody's clinical judgment got quietly overridden by a colour. These five cards are the practical side of getting useful, and none of them involve building anything.

LiveEarning the medical department's trust4 min▶

Three rules, in order of how much difference they make:

  • Bring them a question, not a dashboard. A dashboard is a claim that you already know what they need. A question - "what do you currently have to count by hand?" - is an offer. The first useful thing you build should be something they named.
  • Be visibly right about something small. Not a risk model. Something like: their return-to-training dates and the coach's availability list disagree on four players, and here is which four. Small, checkable, and it saves someone an argument on Friday.
  • Never surprise them in front of the coach. This is the one that actually broke the relationship last time. Anything of yours that touches availability goes to the medical lead before it goes in a meeting, every single time, with no exceptions and no "I thought you had seen it".

Trust here is not won with a better model. It is won by being the analyst who never once made a clinician look uninformed in a room.

LiveWhen your number disagrees with a clinician3 min▶

It will happen. Your picture says a player has had the heaviest 28 days in the squad; the physio says he is fine. Here is the whole protocol:

  • They decide. Not as a courtesy and not as an org-chart formality. They have examined the player, they carry the professional and legal responsibility, and they have information you are not entitled to see.
  • The disagreement is the interesting data. Write it down: what your picture showed, what they said, what happened. Do it every time, without arguing, and after a season you will have the only record in the club of where your exposure view diverges from clinical assessment.
  • Ask one question, not three. "Is there something in the picture that is misleading me?" That is a question about your work. "Are you sure?" is a question about their competence, and you get one of those per career.
  • Never escalate to the coach. Taking a medical disagreement upward through a coach is the fastest way to lose access to medical data permanently, and you would deserve it.
LiveThe wall: availability, not diagnoses3 min▶

There is a hard boundary running through the club's data estate, and analysts sit firmly on one side of it.

  • What you see: is the player available, from when, and how many matches have been missed. Availability is a squad-planning fact.
  • What you do not see: the diagnosis, the imaging, the treatment, the notes, the conversations. Medical detail belongs to the medical department and the player.
  • What you never do: infer the diagnosis from the availability data and write it down. "Out 6 weeks, so it is probably a grade 2 hamstring" is exactly the sentence that ends an analyst's access, and it deserves to.
  • What you never do, part two: put a player's medical detail into a general-purpose AI tool. Athlete health data is among the most sensitive categories a club holds.

The consent, data-rights and governance side of this belongs to the directors: a4 Athlete welfare and data rights covers who may see what, player representation and the regulatory frame. If you are unsure whether you are allowed to hold a field, you are not the person who decides that.

Self-studySpotting the imbalanced-data trap in someone else's model4 min read▶

You need to be able to recognise this from a slide, in a meeting, without the data. Suppose 4 players in a 25-man squad pick up a time-loss injury in a given month. A model that predicts "nobody gets injured" for all 25 is right 21 times out of 25: 84% accurate, and it has found nothing at all.

  • The tell is a high accuracy with no other metric beside it. Ask for recall on the injured group specifically. If the answer is vague, the model is probably predicting the majority class.
  • Ask how many actual injury cases were in the test set. Not the sample size, the case count. Twelve cases is a case study, not a validation.
  • Ask what it does on a season it was not fitted to. The two-number discipline from b1 applies here with more force than anywhere else in the track.
  • Ask whether it beats prior injury alone. Prior injury, ratios often above 2.0, is the baseline. It is also, conveniently, the question that ends the conversation quickest.

This is why low injury incidence is named in the reviews as a direct limit on model performance rather than a technicality. It is not fixable with a cleverer algorithm on a squad of 25.

Self-studyWellness questionnaires and what players know4 min read▶

Most clubs collect a daily self-reported wellness score: sleep, soreness, fatigue, mood. It is cheap, it is quick, and it behaves unlike any other column you hold, for one reason: the player knows who reads it.

  • Self-report is not measurement. The moment a player believes an entry affects selection, the entry becomes a communication rather than an observation. That is not dishonesty, it is a rational response to an incentive you created.
  • Watch for the flattening. A player who returns a 4 out of 5 every single day for six weeks, including the week after a heavy December, is not telling you about his sleep. He is telling you he wants to play.
  • Direction of travel beats absolute level. Comparing one player's scores to another's mostly measures how each of them fills in forms. Comparing a player to his own baseline is at least measuring something consistent.
  • It is medical-adjacent, so treat it as medical. Wellness data is named in the literature as one of the inputs a clinician integrates. It is an input to their judgment, not a column for your ranking.
  • Say out loud what it is used for. If the honest answer is "it feeds the training plan and nothing else", players filling it in honestly costs them nothing, and the data gets better.
Build-along

The availability and exposure picture ★ 20 min · everyone builds it

Rivermouth play eight matches in 24 days between 3 and 27 December, including two two-day turnarounds and a 900 km round trip on the 20th. The head coach has asked "who is going to break down". You are going to hand back something better than an answer to that, and you are going to stop in a very specific place. Reminder: this is orientation for an analyst working alongside clinicians and is not medical advice.

Lay out the block first, before any player data. Eight fixtures, the dates, the turnarounds, the travel. This is a fact about the calendar and it is the only page the coach will look at twice.

Two minutes columns per player: minutes in the last 28 days and in the last 90 days. Raw counts. No ratios, no acute-to-chronic anything, no index.

Add congestion exposure as a count: how many times has this player played three matches in eight days, in his career, as far back as your data goes. A count of a thing that happened, not a rate.

Add availability history: matches missed this season and days since return to full training, taken from the medical department's own records. Availability only. Never a diagnosis, never an inference about one.

Add the one column the literature actually backs: has this player previously had an injury of the same type. Prior injury is the strongest single predictor of future muscle injury, with ratios often above 2.0, and it is the baseline anything fancier has to beat.

Now stop. Do not add a score, a rank, a colour or a combined index. Send it to the medical lead before anyone else sees it, with the supportive-information sentence printed on the page, and let them and the coach ask the next question.

Why stopping is the deliverable Six columns of counted history make the December problem visible: three players have never played three in eight days, one has played every minute since August, and there is no cover at left back. None of that is a prediction, all of it is true, and the people who make the decision now have something they did not have. The moment you add a seventh column that ranks players by risk, everything above it becomes untrustworthy too.
Good prompt - describes exposure, refuses to score 
Prepare a squad availability and exposure picture for a congested fixture
block. Use ONLY these columns from the attached table: player, position,
minutes last 28 days, minutes last 90 days, matches missed this season,
days since return to full training, times the player has played 3 matches
in 8 days, prior injury of the same type (Y/N).

Produce: (1) a plain table sorted by minutes in the last 28 days, with no
added columns; (2) three factual observations about exposure, each one
containing a number that appears in the table; (3) three planning
questions for the medical lead and the head coach.

Hard limits, in order of importance:
- Do NOT produce a risk score, risk ranking, traffic-light colour,
  readiness rating or any combined index.
- Do NOT produce a selection recommendation or return-to-play view.
- Do NOT infer, name, guess or hint at any diagnosis, and do not comment
  on whether any return to training was appropriate.
- Do NOT use the words risk, safe, unsafe, ready, fit or breakdown.
- If something cannot be answered from the columns above, write
  "not answerable from this data" & move on.

Everything here is supportive information for clinical and coaching
judgment, never used on its own for selection or return to play.

Note what that prompt is doing. The hard limits are not politeness, they are the decision line from b1 written as instructions, and they are ordered so the most damaging output is forbidden first. The "not answerable from this data" clause is what stops the model filling a gap with something plausible.

Bad prompt - do not send this 
Here is our GPS and minutes data for the squad. Rank these players by
injury risk for Saturday and tell me which three are most likely to
break down, so we know who to leave out.

Every clause of that is a problem. It asks for a prediction the published evidence does not support at the individual-match level; it asks for a ranking, which is a risk score wearing a different hat; it names a specific fixture, which turns it into a selection input; and "who to leave out" is the selection decision itself, handed to a language model. If a coach asks you for this verbally, the answer is not no - it is the three planning questions from Part 2, and the availability picture that answers them.

Homework

Try it yourself - this week ◐ 30-40 min total

  • Build the availability and exposure picture for your next congested block. Six columns, counted history only, and stop where the build-along stops. Show it to nobody until the medical lead has seen it.
  • Find out whether any model in your club beats the prior-injury baseline. Build the baseline yourself if you have to - it is one column - and ask for the comparison on a season neither was fitted to.
  • Ask your medical lead one question and write down the answer without arguing. Suggested question: "what would an analyst have to do for you to find their work useful?" Then say thank you and leave. While you are there, check whether anything you currently produce contains, implies or allows the reconstruction of a diagnosis.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · A vendor shows you an injury model with 91% accuracy on your league. What do you ask first?

Injuries are rare, so accuracy is close to meaningless: a model predicting that nobody gets hurt scores extremely well on it. The case count and the recall on the injured group tell you whether it found anything, and prior injury - ratios often above 2.0 - is the baseline it has to clear before it has earned a place.

2 · The head coach asks you, on Thursday, which three players are most likely to break down on Saturday. What do you deliver?

Labelling a risk ranking as an estimate does not stop it functioning as a selection input, and refusing outright leaves a real planning problem unanswered. The exposure picture describes history accurately, answers the question the coach is actually trying to settle, and stays on the right side of the decision line. Outputs are supportive information integrated with clinical judgment, never used in isolation for selection or return to play.

3 · Your picture shows a player with the heaviest 28-day minutes load in the squad. The physio says he is fine to start. What do you do?

The clinician has examined the player, holds information you are not entitled to see, and carries the responsibility. Routing a medical disagreement through the coach is how analysts lose access to medical data permanently. Recording the disagreement without arguing is both respectful and the most useful thing you can do: over a season it becomes the only record of where your exposure view and clinical assessment diverge.
